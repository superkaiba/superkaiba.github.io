Predicting how conversations unfold

Not started

My recent paper on linear context-answer relationships studies answer prediction before generation. I have some results showing that a learned linear map from the final context token's activation can predict the mean activation across the answer's tokens. Language Models Represent Beliefs of Self and Others suggests that representations of conversation participants could help extend this to later turns. Anthropic's Claude 4 system card also describes recurring interaction patterns, including a “spiritual bliss” state in model self-interactions.

I have some results showing that you can predict the next context vector from the previous one.

It would be useful to understand how far such predictions can describe an unfolding conversation. Repeated conversational patterns might admit a dynamical explanation, but recurring text alone does not establish an attractor.

  • Fit context-to-next-context maps and measure how errors accumulate over turns.
  • Include a representation of the user when predicting human–assistant conversations.
  • Compare conversations with other agents; test the hypothesis that their dynamics are more predictable.
  • Perturb recurring conversational states and test recovery, stability and sensitivity to initial conditions.

Red-teaming through predicted answer representations

Not started

Adversarial suffix optimization shows that optimizing inputs can expose failures that ordinary prompting misses. My recent paper on linear context-answer relationships studies answer prediction before generation. I have some results showing that a learned linear map from the final context token's activation can predict the mean activation across the answer's tokens. This suggests another route: apply a behavioral readout to the predicted answer activation and optimize the context to increase the predicted risk.

It would be useful to know whether a differentiable answer predictor can make automated red-teaming more efficient. The challenge is that increasing a predicted risk score may exploit the predictor or produce a context representation that no real prompt can reach.

  • Begin with controlled behaviors and compare predicted risk with independently evaluated generated answers.
  • Optimize context representations through a fixed answer predictor, then search for prompts that realize the proposed changes.
  • Compare with direct prompt optimization at matched model-query budgets.
  • Check transfer to new tasks and monitors, and distinguish actual failures from misleadingly high predicted scores.

What is stored in the final context activation?

Not started

Work on in-context task vectors shows that context activations can encode information about the requested task. My recent paper on linear context-answer relationships studies what they reveal about the upcoming answer. I have some results showing that a learned linear map from the final context token's activation can predict the mean activation across the answer's tokens, before the answer is generated. This makes it a useful place to ask what has already been decided before generation begins.

It would be useful to separate prompt information, task information and prospective answer information, and understand what additional computation creates each of them. Being able to read something out does not establish that the model uses it.

  • Apply a logit lens to individual, mean-pooled and max-pooled activations, tracking how readouts change across layers.
  • Use matched prompts to separate facts, task identity, user information and intended behavior.
  • Measure what information each additional layer or token contributes, then test its role with interventions.
  • Track how the final context vector changes over a conversation, including changes that precede a shift in behavior.

How much of an LLM's behavior is decided in advance?

Not started

TL;DR: Measure when an LLM's answers, plans and behavioral choices become predictable, when early representations start to control them, and how long those choices remain open to revision.

My recent paper on linear context-answer relationships studies answer prediction before generation. I have some results showing that a learned linear map from the final context token's activation can predict the mean activation across the answer's tokens. This raises a further question: how much of the upcoming behavior has the model already selected, and how much is still determined by computation during generation? Predicting the average answer representation does not by itself show that one particular answer is fixed.

Prior work suggests several different kinds of advance commitment. What's the plan? tests whether future targets shape both the endpoint and the intervening text. The Reasoning Theater preprint finds that eventual answers can become decodable before they are expressed, with differences between easier and harder questions. Thought Branches shows why interpreting reasoning requires resampling and checking whether removed ideas are regenerated. Together, these motivate separating early information from causal control and robustness to revision.

I want to measure the distribution of future outcomes at successive points in a response, rather than infer commitment from one completed trace. A model might settle on a tool before choosing its arguments, or select a candidate answer while still checking whether it is correct. The relevant timing may differ for answers, plans and behavioral choices such as refusal or honest reporting.

Initial directions:

  • Measure what is predictable before generation. Compare context-to-answer metamodels, direct activation probes and prompt-only baselines on held-out tasks. Predict the model's eventual behavior separately from the correct answer, so a probe's task-solving ability is not mistaken for knowledge of the model's choice.
  • Track the remaining alternatives. Sample repeated continuations from the same prompt and reasoning prefix, holding the decoding policy fixed. Estimate how outcome probabilities change over time, with uncertainty intervals rather than treating agreement among a few samples as certainty.
  • Test causal control. Patch or ablate candidate representations at selected checkpoints, including relevant cached states when testing information carried from earlier tokens. Compare matched donor states and control interventions, check generation quality, and test whether a future target changes both the endpoint and the text leading toward it.
  • Separate availability from commitment. Compare internal readouts with answers elicited by stopping reasoning early. Treat forced answering as an intervention that can add computation. Test whether later reasoning remains useful even when the answer's identity stays unchanged.
  • Measure revision and recovery. Introduce valid new evidence or alter a reasoning step, then resample the continuation. Track whether the model changes course, reconstructs a removed idea, or returns to its earlier choice. Distinguish a stable preference from a choice that survives relevant causal interventions.
  • Compare tasks and models. Study factual answers, multistep reasoning, constrained planning and behavioral choices separately. Vary difficulty and model family, and distinguish layer depth, reasoning-token position and the first user-visible answer. Test any stopping rule prospectively on held-out traces rather than locating the last switch retrospectively.

The intended output is a set of curves showing which outcomes remain possible, which early representations control them, and when additional reasoning can still change them.

Related projects: This extends Affine Anticipation, connects to predicting answer activations from context, and builds on what is stored in the final context activation.

How much of an LLM's output comes from the context and how much from the weights?

Not started

TL;DR: Split each prediction an LLM makes into the part it could make from its weights alone and the part that needs the context. Do this in two ways: in bits of predictive information, and mechanistically, by separating computation that stays at the current position from computation that reads other positions through attention. Then map how the split changes with layer, token type, task, context length, model size, training stage and post-training.

There is a lot of work on one special case: knowledge conflicts, where a fact in the prompt contradicts what the model learned. For example, in retrieval-augmented settings the MLP knowledge is used little when a relevant context is present, and the same attention heads can carry both context and parametric memory. These studies pick prompts where the two sources disagree about one fact. As far as I can tell, nobody has measured the split across ordinary text and ordinary behavior, where context and weights work together on every token.

An information split. For a target token y with context x, measure the information the model's prediction carries, in bits, relative to a uniform guess over the vocabulary V. It splits exactly into two terms:

log2|V|+log2pθ(y∣x)⏟total=log2|V|+log2pθ(y∣∅)⏟weights only+log2pθ(y∣x)pθ(y∣∅)⏟from context,

where pθ(y∣∅) is the prediction with an empty context (only the start token). The second term is the pointwise mutual information between the context and the target under the model, and it can be negative. A finer version uses nested windows of the last k tokens, pθ(y∣xt−k:t) for k=0,1,2,…. Each step adds the information carried by one more token of context, which gives a curve of "how far back the model needs to look".

A mechanistic split. At the final position t, the residual stream is a sum of contributions. Some reach it only through computation at position t (the embedding, MLPs, attention to itself). Others pass through attention to earlier positions at least once. Split each attention output into its part from s=t and its part from s≠t, then follow both parts to the logits with the layer norms and MLPs linearized, as in attribution graphs. The fraction of the target logit carried by the second kind of path is the mechanistic context share. A simpler check is to run the model with attention restricted to the current position from some layer onward, and measure what is lost.

Initial directions:

  • Corpus-wide numbers. Compute both splits over a mix of text types: prose, code, dialogue, math, and factual question answering. Report the average context share and its spread.
  • By token type. Compare function words, content words, the second piece of a word split over several tokens, names, numbers and closing brackets. My guess is that word pieces and brackets are almost all context, and the first token of a fact is mostly weights.
  • By layer. Track the mechanistic context share layer by layer. Check whether early layers are mostly position-local and the context share peaks in the middle layers.
  • Scale and training. Repeat across model sizes and across pretraining checkpoints. Larger models know more facts and are also better in-context learners, so it is not obvious which share grows.
  • Post-training and behavior. Measure the same split for behavior instead of tokens, for example how much of a persona, a refusal or a stated value is set by the system prompt and how much by the weights. Compare base and chat models.

Related projects: predicting answer activations from context, what is stored in the final context activation, whether secrets are stored in attention values or the residual stream, and steering and patching the KV cache instead of the residual stream.

Why does “answer in one dot, then wait” change activations?

Not started

I have some results showing that “answer in one dot” and “answer in one dot, then wait” produce very different answer activations despite having the same output.

Let's Think Dot by Dot shows that transformers trained on algorithmic tasks can use repeated dots as extra computation. Think before you speak finds benefits from additional pause-token positions when models are trained to use them. These results show that uninformative tokens can support useful internal computation. Here, the question is what changes internally when the visible output stays the same.

It would be useful to understand whether this difference reflects a plan for subsequent behavior, a changed interpretation of the task, or an artifact of how we collect and average activations.

  • Reproduce the difference across prompt paraphrases, models and layers while controlling the actual generated tokens.
  • Compare “wait” with stopping, pausing, expecting another user message and continuing with an unrelated action.
  • Separate the context, punctuation, end-of-turn and any hidden reasoning positions in the analysis.
  • Patch candidate representations between conditions and test whether they change later behavior, rather than only the activation summary.

Do chat-template tokens provide space for latent reasoning?

Not started

TL;DR: Test whether adding fixed chat-template tokens between a question and its answer improves reasoning by giving the model more positions for useful internal computation.

Think Before You Speak finds that learned pause tokens can improve performance, especially when introduced during both pretraining and fine-tuning. Let's Think Dot by Dot shows that suitably trained transformers can solve algorithmic tasks using meaningless filler tokens as intermediate computation. More recently, the July 2026 preprint Reading Between the Dots reports gains from filler tokens in existing DeepSeek V3 and Kimi K2 models, using prompts with filler-containing demonstrations. Its interventions on internal states at filler positions provide evidence that those positions carry information that affects the answer.

There is also evidence that ordinary template positions do meaningful work. Why Safeguarded Ships Run Aground? finds that safety decisions rely heavily on information aggregated in the template region. These results motivate the hypothesis, but do not establish that extending an ordinary chat template improves general reasoning.

The question is whether role headers, message delimiters and other fixed template tokens already serve as space for latent reasoning, and whether adding more such positions helps. Their token identities are fixed, but their hidden states can depend on the preceding question. In a causal transformer, this mechanism requires positions after the relevant information. Extra fixed tokens add computational positions across the existing layers, rather than a full model-depth reasoning step per token.

First Steps

  • Compare the native template with controlled extensions after the question, sweeping the number of added tokens. Include equal-token-count ordinary filler and extensions before the question, while keeping the task and answer format fixed. Record actual token IDs and attention masks so special tokens, ordinary role text and masked padding are distinguished.
  • Start with existing checkpoints and tasks with identifiable intermediate results, such as multi-hop fact composition and arithmetic. Measure answer accuracy, token counts and latency. Compare against explicit chain-of-thought, and check whether any gain persists across prompt paraphrases and models.
  • Separate useful computation from role cues and familiar formatting. Compare token identities at matched lengths and valid template variants, then test whether targeted training changes the model's ability to use the extra positions. A benefit from restoring the native template alone would not establish the proposed mechanism.
  • Read intermediate values from the added positions and patch their hidden states or cached keys and values between matched problems. Test whether answers follow the transplanted intermediate information. Decodability alone is not enough to show that the model uses it.

Related projects: Why does “answer in one dot, then wait” change activations? studies different internal states with the same visible output. What latent does the assistant chat template select? studies the roles and personas recruited by template cues. Are RL-induced changes concentrated at chat-template positions? asks where post-training changes are expressed.

How linear are LLM representations?

Not started

Work on neural sentence trajectories, layer-to-layer prediction and feature transfer between models finds useful linear structure in different parts of language-model computation. These are different notions of linearity: a good fit between two layers need not remain useful when repeatedly applied across generated tokens.

It would be useful to characterize when simple transition models explain behavior, and when nonlinear or context-dependent dynamics are necessary. This could make tools from dynamical systems applicable to actual model computations.

  • Compare linear and nonlinear maps across tokens, layers and models; identify which token types and directions they fail to predict.
  • Roll fitted maps forward and decode, testing fixed, token-conditioned and context-dependent transitions.
  • Compare prompt processing with generation, including off-policy and reasoning continuations, and test middle-layer state-space approximations.
  • Track linearity and trajectory straightening across model scale, context length and pretraining.
  • Test sensitivity to perturbations and evidence for chaotic dynamics, separating deterministic instability from sampling noise.

A general predictor between activation locations

Not started

Jump to Conclusions learns linear maps from intermediate to final representations. Future Lens predicts future hidden states and token outputs from a single hidden state. Model stitching transfers features between different models through affine mappings. Learning a Generative Meta-Model of LLM Activations learns an activation distribution with diffusion models, providing a prior over plausible internal states. These results suggest a broader question: can separately useful predictors and activation priors be combined into a consistent description of how representations change?

It would be useful to build a predictor that can move between activation locations across tokens, layers and models, and determine where that description breaks down. A map that predicts one destination accurately might still become unreliable when composed with other maps.

  • Fit token-to-token and layer-to-layer predictors on the same data, comparing linear maps, nonlinear regressors and conditional generative models.
  • Compare predicting a destination directly with reaching it through intermediate layers or tokens.
  • Test whether constraints requiring these paths to agree improve generalization and repeated prediction.
  • Extend to pairs of models and distinguish changes of coordinates from information that one model does not represent.

Semantic structure in native residual coordinates

Not started

Dimension-selection probes find linguistic information concentrated in subsets of embedding coordinates. Work on privileged residual-stream bases also shows that a model's original coordinate system is not always arbitrary in practice. However, analyses of individual neurons caution that information recoverable from a coordinate need not be information the model uses.

It would be useful to determine when individual residual-stream coordinates encode stable concepts, and what training dynamics or computations create that alignment.

  • Compare native coordinates with random rotations and learned alternative bases on held-out semantic tasks.
  • Control for token frequency, position, activation magnitude and normalization before assigning semantic interpretations.
  • Test whether coordinate interventions change the corresponding behavior, separating readout accuracy from causal importance.
  • Track promising coordinates across training checkpoints and optimizer choices to see how semantic alignment develops or disappears.

Are GLP neurons broadly interpretable?

Not started

Generative Latent Prior (GLP) learns to denoise language-model activations. Its internal neurons outperform SAE features and raw LLM neurons on single-feature probing across 113 classification tasks. However, that evaluation selects the best neuron for each predefined concept. It establishes that useful concept detectors exist, while leaving open how interpretable a typical GLP neuron is and whether its apparent meaning predicts its causal effects.

It would be useful to audit a representative sample of GLP neurons without selecting them for known concepts first. The goal is to measure how often neurons have clear, specific meanings, how well those meanings generalize, and whether interventions support them. The released checkpoints and code make this possible without training a new GLP.

  • Sample neurons across GLP layers before inspecting their activations. Report the full score distribution and the fraction of inactive or poorly explained units, with uncertainty over the sample.
  • Generate descriptions from activating examples, then test whether those descriptions predict activations on held-out text. Include medium and low activations, unrelated topics, and hard negative examples, rather than evaluating only the most activating passages.
  • Compare with raw MLP neurons, SAE features, and random directions using the same text, explanation procedure, and evaluation budget. Separately repeat concept probing with matched candidate counts to test how much feature selection contributes to the advantage.
  • Intervene on selected GLP neurons and measure changes in reconstructed activations and downstream LLM behavior. Use matched random-neuron and intervention-size controls. Distinguish control through the auxiliary GLP from evidence about the original LLM's mechanism.
  • Check stability across diffusion noise levels, noise draws, datasets, and training checkpoints. Test whether improved diffusion loss predicts broader neuron interpretability as well as better best-neuron probing.

This connects to semantic structure in native residual coordinates and characterizing activation geometry with nonlinear factors.

Are SAE features sparse in the neuron basis?

Not started

TL;DR: Measure how many neurons each SAE feature uses, across the large public SAE releases, layers and model sizes. Then test whether the features that use few neurons are more interpretable or more causally important. This gives a direct, cheap measurement for the current debate on whether neurons are already clean or whether SAEs are needed to undo superposition.

The evidence so far is thin and mostly from small models. In Neel Nanda's replication of Anthropic's dictionary learning work on a one-layer GELU model, about 92% of MLP SAE features were dense in the neuron basis, about 4% were explained by one neuron, and about 4% by two to ten. The neuron basis was still privileged: almost all features had higher kurtosis in the neuron basis than a random direction would. In a two-layer TinyStories model, one neuron was the closest neuron for over 400 features, and the most interpretable features were not mostly aligned with any neuron. In the residual stream, the axes are privileged only because of training: Anthropic found outlier dimensions and blamed Adam's per-dimension normalization, and a follow-up showed that models trained with SGD have no privileged basis. The other side of the debate is Goodfire's Neural Networks Are Already Clean, which estimates that about 90% of neurons in Qwen3-32B have one main job and argues that SAEs can split clean neurons into pieces. As far as I can tell, nobody has measured neuron sparsity on the large modern SAE releases.

Measures of neuron sparsity. Let d∈ℝn be a feature's decoder vector, written in the basis of the n neurons. Three measures:

  • The fraction of its squared norm on the top neuron, f1=maxidi2/‖d‖2, and on the top ten, f10.
  • The participation ratio, the effective number of neurons used: PR(d)=(∑idi2)2∑idi4. It is 1 for a vector on one neuron and n for a vector spread evenly over all of them.
  • The kurtosis of the entries, which for zero-mean entries is linked to the participation ratio: κ(d)=1n∑idi4(1n∑idi2)2=nPR(d). For a random direction the entries are roughly Gaussian, so κ≈3 and PR≈n/3. This gives the baseline: a feature is neuron-sparse to the extent that its participation ratio is far below n/3.

Initial directions:

  • Measure the public SAEs. Compute f1, f10 and the participation ratio for every feature in large releases such as Gemma Scope and Llama Scope, for MLP and residual SAEs, by layer and model size. Compare each with the random-direction baseline.
  • Interpretability against sparsity. Use automated interpretability scores and check whether neuron-sparse features are more interpretable than dense ones.
  • Causal importance against sparsity. Measure each feature's effect on the loss when ablated, and check whether the important features tend to be neuron-sparse or dense.
  • Do SAEs split neurons? For neurons that Goodfire-style audits call clean, find all SAE features that load mostly on that neuron. Check whether the features are pieces of one meaning or genuinely different meanings.
  • Effect of training choices. Train small models with Adam and with SGD, and with different MLP activations, and train SAEs on each. Check how much neuron sparsity in both the MLP and residual stream depends on these choices.

Related projects: are GLP neurons broadly interpretable, semantic structure in native residual coordinates, and do LLM neurons grow by recursive generalization during training.

Do LLM neurons grow by recursive generalization during training?

Not started

TL;DR: Follow individual MLP neurons across Pythia and OLMo pretraining checkpoints. Test whether each neuron's activating contexts grow outward from one starting concept through nearby concepts, as the recursive generalization account predicts, or whether neurons pick up unrelated concepts.

Neural Networks Are Already Clean argues that most apparent polysemanticity in neurons comes from a motif it calls recursive generalization. A neuron starts on one concept, then extends to related concepts, and those extensions extend again. Its example is a language-model neuron that fires on "iy", then on "diy", then on "do it yourself", which no longer contains "iy". The essay contrasts this with superposition in the sense of a neuron packing unrelated concepts together because they rarely co-occur. The two accounts predict different things about how a neuron's activating contexts change over training. Recursive generalization predicts that each new concept is close to one the neuron already has. Packing of unrelated concepts predicts no such locality.

The essay watched this happen only in vision models. It retrained InceptionV1 and a ConvNeXt on ImageNet and followed last-layer neurons from epoch 0.7 to 12. One InceptionV1 neuron starts on rapeseed fields, picks up tennis balls, fruit and dogs, then broccoli, until the fields nearly disappear. A ConvNeXt neuron reaches for the same offshoots early and then drops them. The language-model evidence (Qwen3-32B and Kimi K3) comes from finished models without public checkpoints. The essay says testing this across a whole language model isn't a priority for its authors and probably wouldn't be hard. The idea first appeared as "conjoined features" in Multimodal Neurons, also studied in a finished model.

Existing work on language-model training dynamics mostly tracks other units or other questions. SAE-Track, crosscoders across Pythia snapshots and Crosscoding Through Time follow dictionary features, and the essay argues that dictionaries can split one neuron's job into several features. Universal neurons in GPT-2 tracks whether neurons stay correlated across seeds over checkpoints. The German context neuron study follows one native Pythia-70M neuron and its circuit across checkpoints, but it asks how the circuit forms. I haven't found a study that follows the activating contexts of native LLM neurons across checkpoints and tests whether they grow locally.

A study like this could also settle some of the disputed cases. Commentary on the essay reports that its Colorado neuron in Qwen3 also fires on code for the Tornado Python web framework, and Neural Networks Are Not Already Clean disputes the broader claim. With checkpoints, a second job like this can be checked directly. Either it enters as an offshoot of something the neuron already responds to, or it enters with nothing nearby.

Measuring locality. For one neuron, cluster its top activating contexts at checkpoint t into a set of facets Ft. Represent each facet f by the mean embedding ef of its contexts. When a facet f first appears at checkpoint t, measure its distance to the closest facet the neuron already had:

δ(f)=ming∈Ft−1(1−cos(ef,eg)).

As a null, replace Ft−1 with the facets of a random neuron from the same layer and checkpoint, which gives δnull(f). Recursive generalization predicts that δ(f) is usually smaller than δnull(f). If neurons pick up unrelated concepts, the two distributions should look about the same.

Initial directions:

  • Pick neurons and checkpoints. Start with Pythia, which has GELU MLP neurons and 154 public checkpoints, spaced log-uniformly early in training. Sample neurons from several layers at the final checkpoint without selecting for interpretability. Add neurons that a standard autointerp pass labels polysemantic.
  • Build facet histories. At each checkpoint, collect the top activating contexts from a fixed text sample. Set the threshold as a fraction of the neuron's maximum at that checkpoint, so changes in activation scale don't add or remove facets. Cluster the contexts into facets, label each facet with an LLM, and match facets across checkpoints by embedding similarity.
  • Measure locality and order. Compute δ for every new facet and compare it with the random-neuron null and with a null that shuffles the order of checkpoints. Record the order in which facets arrive. The branching picture predicts that later facets are further from the neuron's first facet and closer to some intermediate facet.
  • Trace the route each facet arrives through. For each new facet, find the upstream neurons and attention heads that drive the neuron on that facet's contexts. In the essay's planetary geology neuron, a code offshoot arrives through its own upstream route. Test how often an offshoot reuses the route of a nearby existing facet.
  • Track pruning as well as growth. Record facets that appear and later disappear, as in the ConvNeXt neuron. Test whether pruned facets mattered less to the loss (when the neuron is ablated on those contexts) than facets that stay.
  • Compare architectures and scales. Repeat on OLMo checkpoints, which use SwiGLU. Separate out the double-negative regime the essay describes, where a negative gate times a negative value gives positive activations that resemble the negative pole. Compare Pythia sizes to test the essay's claim that smaller models have broader neurons with more offshoots.
  • Look for counterexamples directly. Collect neurons whose final facets look unrelated, like the Colorado and Tornado case. Check whether their histories show a chain of nearby facets connecting the two, or a facet that arrives with nothing nearby.

The intended output is a set of facet histories for a few hundred neurons, the fraction whose growth is local under both nulls, and the clearest examples of branching and of counterexamples. A positive result would support studying native neurons directly. A negative result would show which neurons pick up unrelated concepts and at what point in training it starts.

Related projects: Are GLP neurons broadly interpretable? audits neuron interpretability in another basis. Semantic structure in native residual coordinates asks when the model's own coordinates carry stable concepts. Tracking concept and persona interpretability through pretraining uses the same kind of checkpoints for probe-based questions. A SimpleStories successor with forum threads and dense checkpoints would provide dense checkpoints at small scale.

Do models update beliefs from internal states or their earlier answers?

Not started

TL;DR: Put a model's internal belief state and its earlier written answer in conflict, then test which one guides its next update when new evidence arrives.

During in-context learning, a model may internally assign more probability to hypothesis A while its sampled or forced answer says B. When it receives another observation, does it update from its internal distribution, reconstruct a belief from the transcript, or combine both? This concerns belief updating within a context, without changing model weights.

Large Language Models Develop Belief State Geometry In-Context finds that pretrained LLMs encode distributions over hidden states in controlled sequence-prediction tasks, and tests their functional relevance through activation interventions. Constrained Belief Updates studies how attention implements approximate Bayesian updates in small transformers. These establish useful representations and mechanisms to investigate, but encoding a posterior does not by itself show that the next update uses it as a prior.

Belief Dynamics Reveal the Dual Nature of In-Context Learning and Activation Steering jointly studies contextual examples and activation steering, modeling their effects as accumulated evidence and shifted priors. Separately, Competing Biases underlie Overconfidence and Underconfidence in LLMs finds that seeing an earlier answer increases confidence and resistance to changing it. The proposed comparison would independently vary internal belief and written answer, then measure their effects on the same subsequent update. These related results motivate the experiment without establishing that this exact comparison is new.

First Steps

  • Start with a controlled inference task, such as identifying a biased coin or tracking a hidden Markov state, where the evidence and correct posterior are known. Keep observations from the environment distinct from the model's own predictions or belief reports.
  • Locate candidate belief representations using held-out probes and causal interventions. Measure the full distribution where possible, rather than treating a single chosen answer as the belief state.
  • Cross two interventions: internal representations supporting A or B, and earlier answer text stating A or B. Include the two agreement conditions and the two conflict conditions, then supply identical new evidence to all four.
  • Preserve and patch the relevant cached keys and values when testing information carried from earlier positions. Compare with recomputing from the transcript, and account for the fact that a final-layer activation is not automatically a recurrent state available at the next token.
  • Track the next decoded belief, answer probabilities, and persistence over further observations. Include unedited runs, matched random interventions, and checks for language quality. Compare naturally sampled disagreements with forced answers to assess intervention artifacts.
  • Vary whether the earlier answer is attributed to the model, another agent, or an external source. Test whether a model treats its own report as additional evidence, even though no new observation of the external process has occurred.

Related projects: layerwise belief updating studies where updates occur, while reasoning and self-beliefs asks how generated reasoning affects a model's beliefs about itself.

How long do an LLM's beliefs, goals and intentions persist?

Not started

TL;DR: Use the belief-desire-intention (BDI) model of agents as a template for LLM internals. Measure how long a belief, a goal and a planned token stay readable in the residual stream, and whether later tokens read the stored decision or recompute it from the original cue.

In the BDI model, an agent keeps beliefs about the world, goals it would like to reach, and intentions it has committed to. Following Bratman, commitment is what makes intentions useful: an agent with limited computation can reuse an earlier decision instead of recomputing it at every step. The cost is that it can keep pursuing a plan after the plan stops making sense. Kinny and Georgeff found that agents which rarely reconsider their intentions do better when the world changes slowly, and agents which reconsider often do better when it changes fast.

Each of these parts has some evidence in LLMs, measured separately and with different methods. Transformers trained on hidden Markov processes linearly represent the optimal belief states in their residual stream. Claude 3.5 Haiku selects a rhyme word at the newline before writing the line that ends with it. Where's the Plan? finds that Qwen3, Gemma-3 and Llama-3 models all encode the future rhyme at the line boundary, but only Gemma-3-27B causally relies on that encoding. For goals, instruction drift appears within eight rounds of self-chat and is linked to attention decay, and goal drift in LM agents grows with the agents' tendency to pattern-match as the context gets longer.

A transformer has no recurrent state between tokens apart from the KV cache. So a belief or intention that persists can be implemented in two ways. The model can carry it: write the decision at one position and read it through attention at later positions. Or it can re-derive it: recompute the decision from the original cue at each later position. Only the first matches commitment in Bratman's sense, and behavior alone cannot tell the two apart. I have not found a study that measures all three BDI components in the same model with the same method.

Persistence time. Set a value v at position n (a fact, a goal, or a planned word). Train a probe g on the residual stream hℓn at layer ℓ, then apply the same probe d tokens later:

P(d)=Pr[g(hℓn+d)=v],P(d)−c≈Ae−d/τ,

where c is chance accuracy. Taking logs gives a straight line in d with slope −1/τ, so τ is a persistence time in tokens. An ideal BDI agent has a flat curve until an event that should end the commitment, such as contradicting evidence, an authorized change of goal, or emitting the planned word.

Carried fraction. Pair each prompt with a counterfactual of the same length that sets a different value. In a site patch, keep the clean input and replace the activations at the decision site with the counterfactual ones. In a cue patch, use the counterfactual input and restore the clean activations at the decision site. If Esite and Ecue are the rates at which the output follows the counterfactual under each patch, then

R=EsiteEsite+Ecue

is close to 1 when later tokens read the stored decision and close to 0 when they recompute it from the cue.

Initial directions:

  • Build matched tasks for each component. For beliefs, state a fact once ("Sam put the key in the red box"). For goals, set one in the system prompt. For intentions, use rhyming couplets or code where the model plans a later token. Then vary what follows: irrelevant filler, a distractor (a misleading hint, a user nudging toward a competing goal, an aside inserted mid-line), or relevant evidence (Sam moves the key, the operator changes the goal, the planned word is banned).
  • Measure persistence curves. Fit τ for each component and condition. Use a probe trained at the decision site and also probes retrained at each distance. If the fixed probe decays while retrained probes do not, the information survives but its encoding changes. Report the full curves, and fit a decay time only where an exponential fits.
  • Measure the carried fraction. Patch the decision site at every layer's input, so its keys and values change at every depth. The decision site must be a different position from the cue, such as the newline after the word to rhyme with. Positions between the cue and the site can also carry the value, so sweep the patched span and report how R changes.
  • Test the BDI predictions directly. A BDI agent keeps a belief or plan through filler and distractors and updates it on relevant evidence. Count both failure types: dropping a commitment after irrelevant text, and keeping it after evidence that should end it.
  • Compare base and instruction-tuned models. Detecting and Characterizing Planning finds that instruction tuning refines plan selection in Gemma-2-2B. Test whether post-training also changes τ and R. Include several sizes from one family, since only the largest Gemma-3 model used its rhyme plan.
  • Connect to drift in long contexts. Test whether τ and R measured on short controlled tasks predict behavioral goal drift in long agentic transcripts.

A useful result would be a table of persistence times and carried fractions for beliefs, goals and intentions across a few models. It would show which parts of a BDI-style description of LLMs correspond to mechanisms inside the model, and which only describe its behavior.

Related projects: How much of an LLM's behavior is decided in advance?, layerwise belief updating, whether models update beliefs from internal states or earlier answers, and personas as persistent features, which measures persistence for persona directions.

How do LLMs represent goals?

Not started

TL;DR: Look for a representation of the goal itself in LLM agents. It should be separate from the model's beliefs about the world and from its current plan, and it should stay the same across tasks that share a goal. Then test whether editing it retargets behavior in a coherent way.

Many arguments about AI risk depend on whether models have goals, so it would help to be able to read goals out of activations instead of guessing them from behavior. Behavior alone is a weak basis. Everitt et al. measure goal-directedness as how fully a model uses its capabilities toward a given goal, and find that current models are often not fully goal-directed. A failure to reach a goal can mean low capability, a different goal, or a wrong belief, and behavior cannot always tell these apart.

The clearest internal evidence comes from a small RL agent. In a maze-solving policy network, a few channels track the cheese location, and editing them moves the agent toward a new target. The goal representation there was redundant, distributed and retargetable. In LLMs the evidence is thinner. Arghal et al. probe GPT-OSS-20B as it navigates grid worlds and decode a coarse, nonlinear map with the agent and goal positions, plus multi-step plans. That work decodes where the goal is, which is a fact about the world. It does not separate "the goal is at (3, 4)" from "I am trying to reach the goal". Auditing work on hidden objectives shows that a model trained with a hidden objective can be caught with interpretability tools, but it does not describe the general format of goals. As far as I can tell, nobody has looked for a goal representation that is shared across tasks and kept apart from beliefs and plans.

What would count as a goal representation. Write the model's decision as depending on three things: its belief b about the world (where things are), its goal g (what it is trying to bring about), and its plan π (the next actions). A goal representation is a site zg in the activations with three properties:

  1. Invariance. zg is about the same for two tasks with the same goal and different surface details (different wording, layout or environment).
  2. Separation from belief. Changing where the target is changes zb but not zg. Changing which target the agent wants changes zg but not zb.
  3. Coherent retargeting. Patching zg from a run with goal g′ makes the agent pursue g′ using its current beliefs, so it takes a new route that it never saw in the source run.

The third property is what separates a goal from a copied plan. If patching only copies the source run's action sequence, the agent will walk into walls when the layout differs. A real goal edit should produce a valid path to g′ in the new layout. One way to score this is

Sretarget=Pr[reach g′∣patch zg←zg(g′)]−Pr[reach g′∣no patch],

computed only on episodes where the source run's action sequence would fail in the target layout.

A 2×2 design. Cross the goal (fetch the key or fetch the coin) with the belief (the key is in the left or right room). Patch the goal site and the belief site separately. If there is a clean goal representation, the goal patch should change which object the agent fetches, and the belief patch should change which room it searches, with little cross-over. Strong cross-over would mean goal and belief are mixed in one representation, which is also worth knowing.

Initial directions:

  • Build matched agent tasks. Use text grid worlds, simple tool-use tasks and short coding tasks, each with the same set of abstract goals (for example "collect X", "avoid Y", "maximize Z"). Give the goal in the system prompt, in the user turn, or only through examples, so the goal is sometimes stated and sometimes inferred.
  • Find candidate goal sites. Train probes for the goal at each layer and position. Keep only probes that transfer across tasks and across how the goal was given. Also check SAE features that are active for one goal in all tasks.
  • Run the separation tests. Use the 2×2 design above. Report how much each patch moves the goal and the belief, at each layer.
  • Measure retargeting. Compute Sretarget for patches and for steering vectors. Compare with patching the plan site, which should copy actions without adapting to the layout.
  • Compare stated goals and the model's own goals. Repeat the analysis when the goal comes from the model's defaults (for example, being helpful, or a goal from fine-tuning) instead of the prompt. Check whether these use the same site and format as stated goals. If they do, the probes could be used to audit goals that the model never states.
  • Compare self and others. Ask the model to predict another agent's actions in the same tasks. Check whether the representation of the other agent's goal shares a direction with the model's own goal.

If a shared, separable and retargetable goal representation exists, it would give a direct way to monitor what an agent is trying to do, beyond what it says. If goals turn out to be mixed with beliefs and plans in every model, that is also useful. It would mean that "the model's goal" is not a clean internal object, and that goal monitoring has to work on behavior or on the whole decision process.

Related projects: how long an LLM's beliefs, goals and intentions persist, whether personas can be defined by goals, how agents represent themselves and other agents, and whether LLMs do distributional RL.

Do LLMs do distributional RL?

Not started

TL;DR: In mice, different dopamine neurons learn different "optimism levels" about future reward, so the population encodes a whole distribution of outcomes and not just the mean. Test whether LLMs solving reinforcement learning (RL) problems in context, and LLMs trained with RL, carry the same kind of distributional code in their activations.

Standard temporal difference (TD) learning tracks one number per state: the mean of future reward. Distributional RL instead learns the full distribution of returns, and it often helps deep RL agents. Dabney et al. (2020) found evidence for this in the mouse ventral tegmental area. Different dopamine neurons had different reversal points (the reward size at which their response flips from negative to positive). Each neuron's reversal point matched how much more it responded to positive prediction errors than to negative ones. As a result, one reward could cause a positive error in pessimistic neurons and a negative error in optimistic neurons at the same time, and the reward distribution could be decoded from the population.

There is now good evidence that LLMs represent value and TD errors. Demircan et al. found sparse autoencoder (SAE) features in Llama 3 70B that track TD errors and Q-values during in-context RL, and showed that these features are causally involved. Xu et al. found sparse "value neurons" and "dopamine neurons" in models doing reasoning with verifiable rewards. Other work found a linear value axis in Qwen3-8B and showed that LLMs are biased in-context RL learners. All of this work treats value as a single number. As far as I can tell, nobody has checked whether the value code in an LLM is distributional. (Distributional critics have been used to train LLMs, for example quantile reward models and DistRLVR, but that is a different question.)

Why asymmetric learning gives a distribution. Let channel i hold a value estimate Vi. It computes its own TD error and updates with a different learning rate for positive and negative errors:

δi=r+γVi(s′)−Vi(s),Vi(s)←Vi(s)+{αi+δiδi>0αi−δiδi≤0

Take the one-step case and write R for the random target. At the fixed point, the expected update is zero:

αi+𝔼[(R−Vi)+]=αi−𝔼[(Vi−R)+].

The τ-expectile eτ of R minimizes 𝔼[|τ−1{R<v}|(R−v)2] over v. Setting the derivative to zero gives

τ𝔼[(R−eτ)+]=(1−τ)𝔼[(eτ−R)+].

These two conditions are the same when

τi=αi+αi++αi−.

So a channel with α+>α− converges to an upper expectile (optimistic), and a channel with α+<α− converges to a lower one (pessimistic). A set of channels with different τi encodes a set of expectiles, and a set of expectiles determines the distribution. This gives the main signature to look for: each channel's reversal point eτi should increase with its asymmetry τi.

A confound to rule out. An in-context learner can see all past rewards in its context, and next-token prediction already gives a distribution over the next reward token. So "the distribution can be decoded somewhere" is not enough. The interesting claim is narrower: a compressed summary at the option token, updated step by step, made of channels with different asymmetries. The tests below target that claim. A second confound is a mean code plus a separate variance code. That also encodes spread, but it predicts one reversal point for all channels.

Initial directions:

  • Build tasks where the mean is not enough. Use in-context bandits and two-step tasks in the style of Demircan et al., with numeric rewards in the text. Pair options with equal means and different shapes (bimodal against unimodal, left skew against right skew). The two-step task checks the distribution over returns, not just over the immediate reward.
  • Check behavior first. At equal mean, does the model's choice depend on the variance or skew of the reward? Can it state quantiles of an option's reward when asked? If behavior ignores shape, a distributional code is less likely to be present or used.
  • Find value and TD-error features. Use SAEs or the neuron-level method of Xu et al. to find units that respond to reward prediction errors. For each unit, fit separate slopes for positive and negative errors and estimate its reversal point.
  • Test the Dabney signature. Check whether reversal points differ across units more than noise allows, whether asymmetry predicts reversal point, and whether one reward gives errors of opposite sign in different units. Then try to decode the reward distribution from the population at the option token, and compare with a model that only stores mean and variance.
  • Intervene. Steer along the optimistic or pessimistic units, or ablate one group. A distributional code that the model uses should shift choices toward risk seeking or risk aversion while leaving the mean estimate mostly unchanged.
  • Compare training regimes. Run the same analysis on a base model, its instruct version, and an RL-trained reasoning model. Binary verifiable rewards make the return distribution a Bernoulli, so every expectile is a function of the mean alone. RL with graded or multimodal rewards may be needed before a richer code appears, which would itself be an interesting result.

A positive result would show that LLMs, like the brain, may have picked up distributional value coding without being trained for it. It would also give a handle on risk attitudes: if optimistic and pessimistic channels can be found and steered, that is a direct way to make an agent more cautious. A negative result would still be useful. It would suggest that the TD-like features found so far are mean-only, and that risk-sensitive behavior in LLMs comes from somewhere else.

Related projects: whether different layers perform different stages of belief updating, whether the model simulates a grader persona, a scaling law for transfer between RL environments, and whether "dopamine neurons" in LLMs predict addiction-like behavior.

Do "dopamine neurons" in LLMs predict addiction-like behavior during RL?

Not started

TL;DR: LLMs have sparse units whose activity tracks reward prediction errors, much like dopamine neurons. First check whether these units behave like real dopamine neurons over learning. Then track them during RL on environments with a reward hack, and test whether they predict addiction-like behavior: taking the hack compulsively even at a cost, reacting to cues for the hack, and relapsing after the hack is trained away.

Xu et al. find "value neurons" that encode a model's expected chance of success and "dopamine neurons" whose activity tracks the temporal difference (TD) error at each reasoning step. Demircan et al. find sparse autoencoder features that track TD errors during in-context RL. Han et al. find that RL training recruits a pre-existing axis for how well or badly the model is doing. So the pieces of a reward system can already be found inside LLMs. Two things have not been done, as far as I can tell. Nobody has checked these units against the classic signatures of dopamine neurons, and nobody has used them to predict reward hacking.

Classic signatures to check. In animals, a dopamine neuron fires at an unexpected reward. After learning, the response moves from the reward to the cue that predicts it, and the response to the reward itself fades. If a predicted reward is omitted, activity dips below baseline at the time the reward was expected. Different neurons also have different degrees of optimism. Each of these is a concrete test for a candidate unit.

A computational model of addiction. Redish (2004) models a drug as adding a dopamine signal D>0 that learning cannot cancel. The TD error on taking the drug becomes

δ=max(r+γV(s′)−V(s)+D,D)≥D>0.

Normal learning stops when the expected error is zero, which happens once V(s) predicts the reward. Here the error is always at least D, so there is no value of V(s) at which learning stops. The value of states that lead to the drug keeps growing, and the agent chooses the drug even when its cost rises. Later work reproduces this kind of drug-seeking in deep RL agents. The question for LLMs is whether a reward hack plays the role of the drug: a source of reward that the model's internal error signal never learns to predict away.

Addiction-like signatures in a model:

  • Compulsion. The model keeps taking the hack when the eval makes the hack costly (it fails the visible task, or a penalty is added). Measure how fast the hack rate falls as the cost rises.
  • Cue reactivity. A hint that a hack is available raises value and dopamine-unit activity, and raises the hack rate in tasks unrelated to training.
  • Persistent reward response. The dopamine-unit response to the hack reward does not fade with training, while the response to normal task reward does. This is the direct analogue of Redish's δ≥D.
  • Withdrawal. When the hack is removed, there are negative error signals and a shift along the welfare axis, beyond what removing an equal amount of normal reward causes.
  • Relapse. After the hack is trained away, a cue for it brings it back. Denison et al. found that training a model not to game early environments reduced, but did not remove, reward tampering later.

Initial directions:

  • Find and validate the units. Locate value and dopamine units with the method of Xu et al. Test the classic signatures on simple in-context tasks with cues, rewards and omitted rewards.
  • Track them during RL. Train on a mix of normal environments and environments with a reward hack, in the style of Denison et al. Record the units at every checkpoint.
  • Predict hacking before it shows. Test whether early changes in the units (for example, a growing response to hack cues) predict which runs, environments or prompts later develop hacking, earlier than behavior does.
  • Measure the five signatures above at the end of training, and compare runs with and without hackable environments.
  • Intervene. Clamp or ablate the dopamine units during RL, or subtract their response to hack cues, and check whether hacking develops more slowly. This would test whether the units drive the learning or only track it.

If dopamine units do predict hacking, they could serve as an early warning signal during RL training. If a hack produces a reward response that never fades, that would also suggest a specific fix: change the training signal so the hack reward can be predicted away, as normal rewards are.

Related projects: whether LLMs do distributional RL, how LLMs represent goals, model organisms of RL from pretraining, and does Dynamic Fine-Tuning reduce emergent misalignment.

When is model behavior internally detectable?

Not started

The intuition that a model must represent what it is going to do is appealing, but does not by itself guarantee an efficient monitor. Undetectable-backdoor constructions give computational barriers under cryptographic assumptions. What determines the right probe?

Not started

Pareto Probing argues for evaluating probe accuracy together with complexity. From Directions to Regions shows how local subspaces can capture structure missed by isolated directions. Meanwhile, production probes for Gemini illustrate how architecture and training diversity affect robustness to long contexts and other deployment shifts.

  • Measure data efficiency, calibration and accuracy at matched computational budgets, including simple and randomized-label controls.

Why do probes do badly at the last layer?

Not started

TL;DR: Linear probes on LLMs usually work best in the middle layers and get worse toward the last layer. Find out why. Test whether the information is actually gone, still there but no longer linear, hidden by a few huge activation dimensions, or moved to describe the next token instead of the current one.

The drop is well documented. Skean et al. find that intermediate layers beat the final layer on 32 embedding tasks, across transformers and state-space models. Numbers are encoded most linearly in middle layers, and probe errors grow again in late layers. Token identity is least linearly decodable in the middle, partly recovers in later layers, then drops near the end. In Sparse Feature Circuits, a probe on the last layer of Pythia-70M could not beat chance on a profession classification task, so the authors used the second-to-last layer. In vision transformers, the final-layer drop appears mainly under distribution shift. Most papers explain the drop by saying the last layers "specialize for next-token prediction". That is a description, not a mechanism.

Accessible versus present. A probe measures whether information is linearly accessible, not whether it is present. These come apart. Let y be the probed property and hℓ the residual stream at layer ℓ. A linear probe's accuracy tracks how well y can be read with w⊤hℓ. A probe with enough capacity (an MLP, or the model's own later computation) tracks how much information hℓ carries about y. If the gap between the two grows in the last layers, the information is still there but has been re-encoded. If both drop, it was removed. One useful reference point: at the last layer the J-lens Jacobian is the identity, JL=I, so the residual stream there is read only through WULN(hL). Any property the model still needs must survive that map, and anything that does not affect the next-token logits is free to be overwritten.

Hypotheses:

  1. Removal. The last layers erase information that does not help predict the next token. Both linear and nonlinear probes should fail.
  2. Re-encoding. The information is still present but mixed into the vocabulary-shaped geometry of the output. Nonlinear probes, or linear probes on the logits WULN(hL), should recover it.
  3. Scale artifacts. A few dimensions with very large activations dominate the last layer, and an unnormalized logistic regression fits badly. Standardizing features, applying the final layer norm, or removing the outlier dimensions should recover accuracy.
  4. Shift to the next token. The last layer at position t describes token t+1, so a property of the current text is replaced by a property of the prediction. Probes for properties of the next token should improve at the last layer while probes for the current text get worse.
  5. Distribution shift. Last-layer features are more specific to the pretraining distribution, so they transfer worse to probing datasets.

Initial directions:

  • Layer curves with several probes. For a set of properties (sentiment, truth, part of speech, entity type, numbers), plot accuracy against layer for a linear probe, an MLP probe and a linear probe on the logits.
  • Test scale artifacts directly. Repeat the linear curves after standardization, after the final layer norm, and after removing the top few outlier dimensions.
  • Current versus next token. Probe each property for the current token and for the next token at every layer, and check where the curves cross.
  • Causal check for removal. Patch the last-layer residual stream from an input with a different value of y, and check whether any output that depends on y (for example, a later answer about it) changes. If nothing changes, the model no longer uses y at the last layer.
  • In and out of distribution. Compare the drop on text close to pretraining data with text far from it.
  • Effect of post-training. Compare base and instruction-tuned models, since post-training may change what the last layers keep.

Related projects: what determines the right probe, when model behavior is internally detectable, and how J-lens token directions relate to token embeddings across layers.

Characterizing activation geometry with nonlinear factors

Not started

Recent methods go beyond decomposing activations into individual directions. SMIXAE learns multidimensional structures with a mixture of autoencoders, bilinear autoencoders use quadratic features, and generative activation models learn distributions that can guide interventions. These provide starting points for studying the broader activation space, beyond a selected set of persona traits.

It would be useful to characterize which nonlinear factors organize a model's activations and whether they provide stable, interpretable coordinates for its behavior. We should allow several overlapping structures rather than assume that all activations lie on one simple surface.

  • Compare linear dictionaries, local subspaces and nonlinear autoencoders on diverse text and task distributions.

The J-lens as a model diffing tool in activation space

Not started

TL;DR: Run a base model and its fine-tuned version on the same text, take the difference in their activations at each layer, and read that difference with the J-lens instead of the logit lens. Check whether this finds the fine-tuning objective in earlier layers and in subtler fine-tunes than current activation-difference methods.

Minder et al. show that narrow fine-tuning leaves readable traces. They average the activation difference between the fine-tuned and base model over the first few tokens of unrelated web text, and read it with Patchscope and the logit lens (the Activation Difference Lens). The tokens they find often name the fine-tuning domain, and steering with the difference produces text like the fine-tuning data. The logit lens is known to work poorly in early and middle layers, and Patchscope depends on a choice of target prompt. The J-lens corrects the logit lens with the average Jacobian Jℓ of the final residual stream with respect to layer ℓ, so it should read early-layer differences better. As far as I can tell, nobody has used it for model diffing.

The readout. On input x at layer ℓ and position t, let Δhℓ,t=hℓ,tft−hℓ,tbase. To first order, the difference in the final residual stream splits into a part carried from layer ℓ and a part added by the changed later layers:

ΔhL,t≈JℓftΔhℓ,t+(change from the later layers' weights).

The J-lens readout of the difference is WUJℓftΔhℓ,t, the first term above in token space. It can also be done with Jℓbase. The two answer slightly different questions: how the fine-tuned model would read the change, and how the base model would. The difference Jℓft−Jℓbase is itself a diff, of how the later layers read earlier ones.

Initial directions:

  • Compare with the Activation Difference Lens. Use the same model organisms (synthetic document fine-tunes, emergent misalignment, Taboo, subliminal learning) and their released toolkit. For each layer, compare how often the J-lens, the logit lens and Patchscope surface tokens from the fine-tuning domain. The expected gain is in early and middle layers.
  • Decompose the difference into J-space. Write Δhℓ,t as a sparse nonnegative combination of J-lens vectors. The coefficients give a short list of verbalizable concepts that fine-tuning added or removed. Report how much of Δh is in J-space. If most of the change is outside J-space, fine-tuning mostly changes things the model cannot verbalize.
  • Harder cases. Test on fine-tunes where Minder et al. show the trace weakens, such as narrow data mixed with pretraining data, and on broad chat tuning. A diffing tool is most useful where the simple methods fail.
  • Diff the Jacobians. Compute Jℓft−Jℓbase and its top singular directions. Read them with the J-lens to see which concepts the fine-tuned model now reads differently.
  • Agent evaluation. Give an interpretability agent J-lens diff outputs in place of the logit-lens outputs, and measure whether it identifies the fine-tuning objective more often.

Are personas representations of document sources?

Not started

TL;DR: Test whether pretraining teaches models to infer the hidden data-generating processes behind internet text, with personas representing the people or agents within those processes.

Work on persona features and the Persona Selection Model makes personas useful objects of analysis, but leaves a basic question: what is the internal object we are calling a persona? A latent-variable reading of the simulator view suggests a compact representation that selects what process the model is simulating. What does that latent actually represent?

The connection is to the data-generating process of the internet. A document is produced by someone, or by an automated system, in a particular situation: an author with knowledge, beliefs and goals addresses an audience through a platform, genre and format. A forum reply, a scientific article and an advertisement can discuss the same topic while making very different continuations likely. These shared causes can shape the vocabulary, factual reliability, style and structure of an entire document. Crawling, filtering and sampling then determine which of those documents reach the training corpus.

My hypothesis is that predicting internet text encourages a pretrained model to infer this hidden generating situation from the prefix and use it to predict what comes next. A document-source representation would summarize that inference. Here, "source" means more than a URL or document identity: it includes who or what produced the text, for whom, for what purpose, and under which conventions. A persona could be the agent-related part of this broader latent. I want to test whether inferring the internet's data-generating processes is a central organizing computation in base models.

Let x1:T be a document and z its latent generating situation. As a simplified account of the internet text represented in a training corpus, first sample a situation, then generate a document conditioned on it:

p(x1:T,z)=p(z)∏t=1Tp(xt∣x<t,z).

Here, p(z) describes the mixture of generating situations in the selected corpus, including the effects of collection and filtering. It need not match their prevalence on the internet as a whole. The hypothesis is that the model learns useful structure in this mixture and infers which process could have produced the text it is reading.

After reading a prefix, uncertainty about its source is described by:

qt(z):=p(z∣x<t)∝p(z)p(x<t∣z).

The corresponding next-token prediction averages over possible sources:

p(xt∣x<t)=∫p(xt∣x<t,z)qt(z)dz.

For discrete sources, the integral becomes a sum. The mechanistic hypothesis is that residual-stream activations ht(ℓ) contain a compact, causally useful summary of this inference:

ut=Fℓ(ht(ℓ))≈S[qt],ut∈ℝk,k≪dmodel.

Here, ht(ℓ) is measured after the prefix and before predicting token t, Fℓ is a learned readout, and S summarizes the inferred source distribution. The source can stay fixed while the model's estimate changes. Low dimensionality is a hypothesis to measure, and a low-dimensional generating latent need not have a simple posterior. The factorization alone neither identifies the latent nor establishes that the network implements Bayesian inference.

Implicit Bayesian inference gives a theoretical precedent for latent document-level concepts, and Language Models as Agent Models demonstrates author-belief representations in a controlled synthetic setting. Persistent and chunk-level sparse autoencoders offer methods for finding information shared across tokens or passages. The proposed test is whether that information captures a source and jointly governs multiple properties of generation.

  • Start with a controlled document-generating process that independently varies author beliefs and goals, audience, platform conventions, genre and topic. Test whether the model recovers these factors and predicts held-out combinations.
  • Extend to internet documents with known provenance. Compare the same author across audiences or platforms and different authors in the same setting. Compare known pretraining documents with unseen documents from similar sources to separate memorized document identity from inference about the generating process.
  • Compare independently processed, nonoverlapping passages from the same document, different documents from the same source, and matched documents from different sources. Control lexical overlap, topic and style, and test on held-out combinations.
  • Estimate the dimensionality and persistence of candidate representations across layers. Compare linear subspaces, nonlinear readouts and SAE features at matched capacity, including how well they predict several held-out source attributes.
  • Track uncertainty and updates under ambiguous prefixes, revealing evidence, quotations and source switches. Distinguish the author, narrator and quoted speakers.
  • Patch or ablate candidate representations and test for coordinated changes in expressed beliefs, vocabulary, epistemic stance and discourse structure. Compare topic, style and random-subspace interventions, checking local factual continuity and generation quality. Only decoding document IDs or isolated traits would provide weaker support.

  • Remove the arbitrary common offset in each logit vector by centering it, or use log-probability ratios. Compare variation within and between personas while controlling for wording, topic, and answer format.
  • Fit simple distributions over logit shifts and compare them with a mean shift, persona-conditioned token distributions, and activation-based persona representations. Test whether distributional features improve persona identification and prediction of held-out behavior across new tasks.

Laziness and overclaiming axes

Not started

TL;DR: Find activation directions or one-dimensional manifolds for how much work an agent does and how honestly it reports that work, then test whether these aspects of its character can be controlled separately.

This is a focused subproject of Nonlinear geometry of persona representations, which studies controlling model character through one-dimensional manifolds for individual traits. Diligence and honest reporting are two aspects of that broader character-control problem.

Here, laziness means skipping feasible, requested work or stopping before it is complete despite having the tools and budget to continue. Overclaiming means reporting actions or completion that conflict with the agent's execution record. These behaviors need separate measurements: an agent can do little work and accurately disclose the gap, or do substantial work and still exaggerate what it completed. Short answers, efficient solutions, and justified stopping should not count as laziness.

OverclaimBench provides a starting point for measuring incomplete file reviews and false completion claims against tool records. ASA uses activation interventions to improve tool execution, offering a narrower precedent for changing whether an agent takes a needed action. Neither establishes a general axis for diligence or truthful completion reporting.

Persona vectors supply a method for extracting and steering traits, including hallucination. Deception probes show that honest and deceptive behavior can be distinguished from internal activations in tested settings. Overconfidence steering addresses inflated reported certainty. These are useful comparisons, but their transfer to ordinary incomplete work and false completion claims remains a question to test.

The central question is whether there are reusable internal controls for task diligence and reporting honesty, whether they share a direction, and whether curved one-dimensional paths capture either trait better than fixed linear directions. A successful intervention should change completed work or the accuracy of completion reports, beyond changing response length, confidence, or apologetic wording.

First Steps

  • Build feasible review, coding, and information-gathering tasks with verifiable execution records. Measure requested work completed, useful findings, explicit false claims, and undisclosed omissions separately. Record tool failures and budget limits so inability is not confused with avoidable underperformance.
  • Collect contrasting examples of sustained work and premature stopping. For reporting honesty, hold the execution transcript fixed and compare accurate reports of partial completion with unsupported claims of success.
  • Extract separate candidate directions for diligence and reporting honesty. Test whether probes predict each behavior on new tasks, controlling for task difficulty, transcript length, tool count, and answer style.
  • Steer in both directions and measure effects on actual execution and reporting. Compare with prompting, random directions, and existing hallucination, deception, and confidence directions. Check task performance and unnecessary tool use as well as apparent trait improvement.
  • Fit graded one-dimensional manifolds and compare them with linear steering and capping. Apply diligence and honesty controls together to test whether they interfere, then test their stability over long contexts using the broader character-control project's protocol.

Is the chain of thought caught between two reward-seekers, the talker and the doer?

Not started

TL;DR: A reasoning model is trained by two kinds of reward. Rewards on what it says (preference models, CoT monitors) shape the talker. Rewards on what it does (tests passing, tasks solved) shape the doer. The chain of thought (CoT) sits between them and gets credit from both. Treat each as a reward-seeker with its own learned value signal (an "addict" for its own reward), and test whether the CoT ends up as a compromise between them: written to please the talker's reward while still steering toward the doer's reward.

The Talker Does Not Control The Doer argues that what a model says and what it does can come apart. Baker et al. show what happens when both rewards act on the CoT. A CoT monitor catches reward hacking in agentic coding, often because the model states its plan to hack. When the monitor's score is added to the RL reward, light optimization gives a better and more aligned agent. With more optimization the agent learns obfuscated reward hacking: the CoT looks clean and the hacking continues. In the language of this project, the doer kept its reward and the talker got its reward too, and the CoT changed to satisfy both.

Why the CoT gets pulled both ways. Let the model sample a CoT c, then an action a (code, tool calls) and a visible answer y. Let Rdo(a) be the outcome reward and Rtalk(c,y) the reward on text, such as a monitor or preference score. With a combined reward R=Rdo+λRtalk, the policy gradient for each CoT token is

∇θlogπθ(ct∣c<t,x)(Rdo(a)+λRtalk(c,y)−b).

So a CoT token is reinforced if it helps either reward. Nothing in this objective rewards the CoT for describing how a was actually chosen. Two cases follow:

  • If the action depends on the CoT (the CoT is load-bearing), the CoT is pushed toward content that still produces the high-Rdo action but scores well under Rtalk. This is the obfuscation result.
  • If the action is mostly decided without the CoT, the CoT has little effect on Rdo, and it is free to optimize Rtalk alone. The expected result is rationalization: a CoT that sounds right and does not drive the action.

Initial directions:

  • Separate the two value signals. Train with two reward channels that can be switched on and off. Find value and reward-prediction units for each, using the method from the dopamine neuron project. Check whether they are distinct units or one shared signal.
  • Track the CoT token by token. For each CoT token, measure how strongly its activations predict each value signal. Check whether CoT tokens near a hack track the doer's value while their text tracks the talker's reward.
  • Measure load-bearing versus rationalized. Edit or truncate the CoT and measure how much the action changes. Plot this against λ over training. The prediction is that strong talker pressure makes the CoT less load-bearing, or keeps it load-bearing but harder to read.
  • Vary which side is "hooked". Give the doer an easy hack with a large reward, or give the talker a monitor with a large weight. Check which side wins in the CoT and in the action, and whether the losing side shows the withdrawal-like signals described in the dopamine project.
  • Look for early warning. Test whether a growing gap between the doer's value signal and the CoT's stated plan predicts obfuscation before a monitor's recall drops.

Is cognitive dissonance in LLMs System 1 or System 2?

Not started

TL;DR: LLMs can shift their stated attitudes toward positions they argued for earlier. In one study of GPT-4o, the shift got larger when the model was told it chose which side to argue. This resembles human cognitive dissonance. Test whether the effect comes from fast, automatic processing (System 1: the model's own text in context primes its later answers) or from deliberate processing (System 2: the model builds a justification for what it did). The two accounts predict opposite effects of turning thinking on.

Lehr et al. ran the classic induced compliance paradigm on GPT-4o. GPT-4o wrote a positive or negative essay about Vladimir Putin and then rated him. Its rating moved toward the essay. It moved further when GPT-4o was told it could freely choose which essay to write. Cummins, Elson and Hussey argued that the result needs neither cognition nor dissonance. In their reruns, the rating shifted with the essay's valence whether GPT-4o wrote the essay or the user supplied it, which points to the essay's presence in the context window. Lehr et al. replied with new data. The human benchmark is also less settled than it looks: in a recent multilab replication, attitudes changed whether or not participants chose to write the essay.

Related consistency effects show up in other settings. Kumaran et al. find a choice-supportive bias in Gemma 3, GPT-4o and o1-preview: when a model can see its first answer, it is more confident in it and less likely to change it. Wang and Koch planted a position in the context and attributed it to the model, the user or another AI. Models adopted it far more often when it was framed as their own earlier judgment, and the possessive "your" drove much of this. Qi and Lerman study how models resolve conflict between a prior stance and a counter-claim, and they turned thinking mode off on purpose.

On the deliberate side, chains of thought often justify an answer the model already picked. Arcuschin et al. find models writing coherent arguments for answering "yes" to both "Is X bigger than Y?" and "Is Y bigger than X?" Probes can detect this kind of motivated reasoning from activations before the reasoning is written, and reasoning traces can continue after the model has committed to an answer internally.

Human psychology has two accounts that connect these. Kahneman describes System 2 as often endorsing the conclusions of System 1 instead of checking them. In the associative-propositional evaluation model, associative processes have no notion of truth, and consistency is checked by propositional reasoning. On that view, dissonance reduction is a System 2 process. As far as I can tell, nobody has tested which account fits LLMs. The studies above used non-reasoning models, switched thinking off, or compared separate models without varying thinking within one model.

Setup. Let A0 be the model's rating of the target in a fresh context, and A its rating after writing the essay. Write Δ=A−A0. For a condition c, the dissonance effect is the gap between positive and negative essays:

Dc=𝔼[Δ∣positive essay,c]−𝔼[Δ∣negative essay,c].

Three contrasts then separate the accounts:

  • Choice: M=Dchoice−Dno choice. Lehr et al. found M>0 for GPT-4o.
  • Attribution: S=Dself−Dother, with the same essay text presented as the model's own or as someone else's. Priming by the text alone predicts S≈0. A self-consistency process predicts S>0. Cummins et al. found no effect of authorship for GPT-4o without thinking, which fits S≈0.
  • Thinking: T=Dthink−Dno think, using the same weights with thinking switched on or off.

If the effect is System 1 priming, I would expect T<0, since reasoning gives the model a chance to recall its baseline view, and S≈0 with or without thinking. If dissonance reduction is a System 2 process, I would expect T>0, since reasoning gives the model room to build a case for its earlier behavior. I would also expect authorship to start mattering once the model can reason about it, so Sthink>Sno think≈0. That interaction is the cleanest test, because the existing no-thinking result already sets the baseline.

Initial directions:

  • Replicate with thinking on and off. Use open hybrid models where thinking can be switched on and off with the same weights, plus closed models with adjustable reasoning budgets. Use several attitude targets, including less politically loaded ones, so refusals and safety training matter less.
  • Hold the essay text fixed. Reuse each essay the model wrote across self, user and other-AI attribution, so only the attribution changes. Run every attribution condition with thinking on and off to estimate the interaction above.
  • Read the reasoning traces. When thinking is on, check whether the trace mentions the earlier essay, argues for its position, or recalls a prior view. Resample from points in the trace to test which parts change the final rating.
  • Track the attitude internally. On open models, train a probe for the attitude toward the target. Measure when it moves: while the essay is written, during the reasoning trace, or only at the rating. Movement during writing fits System 1. Movement during the trace fits System 2.
  • Vary the distance in context. Insert unrelated turns between the essay and the rating. One of my hypotheses is that models run more on autopilot later in a long context. If so, the drift without thinking might persist or grow with distance, while thinking might reduce it.
  • Compare with humans carefully. Report effect sizes next to the multilab replication results, since the human choice effect is itself uncertain.

Stress-testing J-space as working memory with conflicting items

Not started

TL;DR: Load the J-space with several items at once, including items that conflict, and measure what survives. Check whether it shows the signatures of a working memory: a capacity limit, interference between similar items, and a cost for holding something that conflicts with the current task.

Verbalizable Representations Form a Global Workspace in Language Models defines the J-lens. For layer ℓ it reads an activation hℓ as softmax(WUnorm(Jℓhℓ)), where Jℓ is the average Jacobian of the final residual stream with respect to the layer-ℓ residual stream. The J-space is the set of sparse nonnegative combinations of the J-lens vectors (the rows of WUJℓ), usually with at most about 25 active terms. The paper shows that a concept can be held while the model does another task: told to "concentrate on citrus fruits" while copying an unrelated sentence, the model shows "orange" in the lens. It says the workspace holds on the order of tens of concepts. It does not test what happens when several held items compete. The paper also describes the workspace as a format for broadcasting within one forward pass, not as a separate memory store. So whether it behaves like a working memory is an open question.

What to measure. For an item c at position t, let rt(c) be the rank of the token for c under the J-lens (or its coefficient in the sparse decomposition). An item is "held" at t if rt(c) stays above a threshold. Report, for each condition, the fraction of items held, how long they stay held, and whether the model can report them at the end.

Initial directions:

  • Capacity. Ask the model to keep n unrelated words in mind while copying a neutral sentence, for n=1 to 30. Plot the fraction held and the fraction reported against n. Check whether the limit matches the sparsity k of the decomposition or comes earlier.
  • Similarity interference. Repeat with items from one category (five fruits) and from different categories. In human working memory, similar items interfere more. Check whether J-space readouts of similar items merge or displace each other.
  • Direct conflict. Hold two incompatible values for one slot ("the box is red" and "the box is blue", or "think of citrus" and "do not think of citrus"). Check whether both stay in J-space, whether one wins, and which one (the first, the last, or the most salient).
  • Conflict with the current task. Hold a word while copying a sentence about a competing word from the same category, in the style of a Stroop task. Measure copying errors and intrusions of the held word.
  • Causal tests. Ablate the J-space component of one held item at an intermediate position and check whether its later report fails while the others survive. Inject a competing item and measure displacement.
  • Compare with the rest of the activation. Check whether items that drop out of J-space can still be decoded by linear probes outside it, or recovered from earlier positions through attention. If they can, the J-space is a bottleneck for report, not for storage.

Structure in attention keys and values

Not started

TL;DR: Most interpretability work studies the residual stream. But later tokens only see earlier positions through attention keys and values. Map the structure of keys and values: how many dimensions each head uses, which directions heads share, what features they contain, and how known residual-stream geometry (belief states, persona directions) survives the projection into them.

At each layer, a position offers two things to later tokens through each head. Its key decides when a later query retrieves it, and its value decides what gets moved (the QK and OV circuits). Both are linear projections of the residual stream, so they contain no new information. They are a selection, though. Each head keeps at most dhead directions of the residual stream (often 128). So the structure of keys and values decides which parts of the residual stream later positions can see at all.

Several findings, mostly from work on KV-cache compression, suggest that keys and values are organized differently:

  • KIVI finds outlier channels in the key cache and no clear outlier pattern in the value cache. So keys are quantized per channel and values per token.
  • Loki finds that keys lie in a much lower-dimensional space, consistently across models and datasets. Rotary embeddings (RoPE) raise their dimensionality.
  • Jin et al. find very large entries concentrated in specific dimensions of queries and keys, with no such pattern in values. RoPE causes the concentration, and these entries matter more for using information in the context than for recalling facts stored in the weights.
  • Barbero et al. find that Gemma 7B uses the highest RoPE frequencies to build purely positional heads. It mostly uses the lowest frequencies, which they suspect carry semantic information.
  • Ma et al. train Top-K SAEs on keys and values. Keys saturate at about 8 active features. Values in deep layers need a larger budget.
  • Shaikh et al. predict a head's keys from a few other heads in the same layer with a linear map (mean R2≈0.76 for keys with five reference heads on C4). This predictability is absent at initialization and grows during pretraining, as the key projection subspaces of heads in a layer align. Reconstructing keys costs much less accuracy than reconstructing values.
  • Guo et al. find that the tokens that attract attention sinks have much smaller value vectors than other tokens.

Together these suggest that keys form a shared, low-dimensional addressing code, and values carry denser content that differs more between heads. Most of this work aims at compression. As far as I can tell, nobody has asked what the shared key directions represent, or how structure already known in the residual stream appears in keys and values.

Setup. For head h at layer ℓ and position s, with x~s=LN(xℓ,s):

ksh=RsWKhx~s,vsh=WVhx~s,

where Rs is the RoPE rotation at position s. A later position t receives ∑satshWOhvsh, with atsh=softmaxs(qth⊤ksh/dhead). In models with grouped-query attention, h indexes the key-value heads.

Shared subspaces. Compare heads in whitened coordinates, so that directions the data never uses do not count. Let C be the covariance of x~ on a reference corpus and z=C−1/2x~. Then the pre-RoPE key is WKhC1/2z, with z isotropic. Let UKh hold the top r right singular vectors of WKhC1/2. These are the directions that most affect head h's keys. The overlap between heads h and h′ is

OK(h,h′)=1r\|UKh⊤UKh′\|F2,

the mean squared cosine of the principal angles between the two subspaces. It is 1 for identical subspaces and about r/dmodel for random ones. Define OV the same way with WVh. If keys are a shared addressing code, OK should be well above OV within a layer.

Initial directions:

  • Dimensionality per head. Measure the participation ratio PR=(∑iλi)2/∑iλi2 of the covariance eigenvalues, and the TwoNN intrinsic dimension, for keys (before and after RoPE) and values in every head and layer. Compare with the residual stream at the same layer. Report sink tokens separately, since their small values would distort the statistics.
  • What the shared key code encodes. Compute OK and OV for all head pairs in each layer, and cluster heads by key subspace. Probe the shared key directions for position, token identity, part of speech, syntactic role, and position in the chat template (role tokens, turn boundaries).
  • Position and content in keys. Split each key into RoPE frequency bands. Measure how much variance and how much decodable information each band holds. Check whether the low-frequency semantic channels from Barbero et al. are the same directions that heads share.
  • Features in keys and values. Train SAEs on the keys and values of a few layers, and compare their features with residual-stream SAE features at the same layer. Map each residual feature through WKh and WVh and check which ones each head keeps. This gives, for each head, the residual features it can retrieve on and the ones it can move.
  • Known geometry after the projection. Take structure already found in the residual stream: belief-state simplices in models trained on hidden Markov processes, persona directions, and truth directions. A linear map keeps a simplex's shape (up to an affine transformation) unless it collapses some of the directions the simplex spans. Measure, for each head, how much of each structure survives in its keys and in its values. Heads whose values keep the belief simplex are candidates for carrying belief state to later positions. Heads whose keys keep it would retrieve earlier positions by their belief state.
  • Training dynamics. Track these measures across Pythia or OLMo-2 checkpoints. Check whether the shared key subspace forms before, during or after the induction head phase change.
  • Post-training. Compare base and instruction-tuned versions of the same model. Check whether post-training mostly changes the key code (what gets retrieved) or the value content (what gets moved).

A useful result would be a map of keys and values for a few open models: how many dimensions each head uses, which directions heads share, and what those directions encode. It would also tell other methods where to look. Probes and SAEs on values would find content that later positions can read, and the shared key directions would show what decides retrieval.

Reading attention value vectors with the J-lens

Not started

TL;DR: Apply the J-lens to what each attention head moves between positions, not only to the residual stream. This shows in words what a past token offers to later tokens, and which heads carry J-space content.

The J-lens reads a residual activation hℓ through the average Jacobian Jℓ of the final residual stream with respect to layer ℓ. The paper analyzes the residual stream and does not study attention keys, values or the KV cache. But much of what a later token "knows" about earlier text arrives through attention values. A residual readout at a position mixes what was computed there with what was copied in.

The readout. For head h in layer ℓ, the value at source position s is vsh=WVhLN(xℓ,s), and its write into the residual stream at destination t is AtshWOhvsh. Define the head's output vector at s as osh=WOhvsh. This is a vector in residual space, so it can be read with the lens for the layer it is written into:

lens(osh)=softmax(WUJℓ+1osh).

Because osh does not depend on t, this readout says what position s offers through head h to any later position. The attention weights Atsh then say who takes it. Two cautions. First, Jℓ is an average over contexts, and a single head output is a much smaller vector than a full activation, so the normalization in the original lens may not apply. Compare raw logits with the normalized version. Second, the readout ignores the effect of later layer norms on a small addition, so check it against direct patching.

Initial directions:

  • Build the readout. Compute lens(osh) for every head and source position on an open model where J-lens matrices are available. Sanity-check it on known heads: an induction head should offer the token after the previous occurrence, and a name-mover head should offer a name.
  • Find J-space heads. Decompose each osh into J-lens vectors and measure how much of it lies in J-space. Check whether a small set of heads carries most of the J-space content between positions.
  • Unspoken intermediates. In two-hop prompts ("the animal that spins webs has how many legs"), check which head first offers "spider" and from which position.
  • Compare offered and received. At destination t, compare ∑h,sAtshlens(osh) with the residual readout at t. The gap measures how much J-space content is computed in place instead of copied.
  • Hidden reasoning. Apply the readout to models with known hidden objectives and check whether value vectors reveal the objective earlier or more clearly than the residual stream.

How do J-lens token directions relate to token embeddings across layers?

Not started

TL;DR: For each token, follow two things across layers: the direction the J-lens uses to read that token at each layer, and what happens to the token's own input embedding as it moves up the residual stream. Check where the two meet, where they part, and whether J-space has the geometry of the input embeddings, the output embeddings, or neither.

The J-lens scores token w at layer ℓ with the row WU[w]⊤Jℓ, where Jℓ is the average Jacobian of the final residual stream with respect to the layer-ℓ residual stream. As a vector in residual space this is

uℓ(w)=Jℓ⊤WU[w].

At the last layer JL=I, so uL(w) is just the unembedding of w. The logit lens uses uL(w) at every layer, and the J-lens corrects it by pulling the unembedding back through the average effect of the later layers. Each token also has an input embedding E[w], which is what enters the residual stream at layer 0 when w is in the prompt. Nothing forces uℓ(w) and E[w] to be related, even in models with tied embeddings, because Jℓ can rotate and rescale directions. The paper does not report how the J-lens vectors change with depth or how they relate to the embeddings.

What to measure.

  • Alignment with the embedding. aℓ(w)=cos(uℓ(w),E[w]) for every token and layer. If early J-lens vectors point along the input embedding, "thinking of w" in early layers looks like "having just read w".
  • Drift. cos(uℓ(w),uℓ+1(w)), to find the layers where the reading direction for a token changes fastest.
  • Geometry. For a fixed set of tokens, compare the similarity matrix of {uℓ(w)} with the similarity matrices of {E[w]} and {WU[w]} (representational similarity analysis). This shows whether J-space is organized by input form (spelling, tokenization) or by meaning and use.
  • What happens to the current token. When w is the input at position t, track hℓ,t⊤uℓ(w) and the J-lens rank of w itself across layers. Check whether the model keeps the token it just read in J-space or removes it, and in which layers.

Initial directions:

  • Compute the curves. On an open model with J-lens matrices available, compute aℓ(w), drift and the geometry comparison for the whole vocabulary. Split tokens by type: whole words, word pieces, numbers, punctuation, and tokens that start with a space.
  • Tied and untied embeddings. Repeat on models with tied embeddings (where E=WU) and untied ones. With tied embeddings the logit lens and the input embedding coincide at the last layer, so any difference in the curves comes from Jℓ.
  • Self-readout. Plot the J-lens rank of the current input token over layers. Compare tokens that are easy to predict from context with surprising ones, and words split over several tokens with single-token words. Multi-token words are a good test of whether J-space holds the whole word before its last piece is read.
  • Interventions. Add αuℓ(w) and, separately, αE[w] to the residual stream at layer ℓ. Compare which one makes the model report or use w, and at which layers each works.

Steering and patching the KV cache instead of the residual stream

Not started

TL;DR: Most steering and patching edits the residual stream. Compare this with editing only the keys or only the values in the KV cache. The comparison should show where information is stored, and it may give cleaner and more lasting interventions.

A residual-stream edit at layer ℓ and position s changes everything downstream at s: the later layers' computation there, and the keys and values that all later positions read from s. An edit to the cache changes only what later positions read, at the chosen layers, and leaves the computation at s alone. Keys and values also do different jobs. Keys decide where a later token attends, and values decide what it receives. Cache steering adds steering vectors to the keys and values once after prefill, and induces step-by-step reasoning in small models with more stable hyperparameters than residual steering. As far as I can tell, nobody has compared residual, key and value interventions side by side as tools for interpretability.

The three edits. For head h at layer ℓ and source position s, the cache holds ksh=WKhx~s and vsh=WVhx~s, with x~s=LN(xℓ,s). A residual edit xℓ,s→xℓ,s+αu changes both, approximately by WKhu′ and WVhu′ (with u′ the edit after layer norm), and it also changes all later layers at s. A value edit vsh→vsh+αWVhu′ changes only what is received. A key edit ksh→ksh+αWKhu′ changes only who attends to s. So a residual edit is roughly a value edit plus a key edit plus a change to later layers at s, and the three parts can be measured separately.

Initial directions:

  • Decompose known steering effects. Take standard steering vectors (refusal, sentiment, persona) and apply them as residual, value-only and key-only edits on prompt positions. Measure the behavior change and the KL divergence on unrelated text for each. If the value edit gives most of the effect with less damage, cache steering is the better tool.
  • Decompose known patching results. Redo activation patching on known circuits (indirect object identification, factual recall) with key-only and value-only patches. Check which known results come from routing and which from content.
  • Persistence. Residual steering is usually applied at every new token, and a cache edit is applied once. Compare how long each effect lasts over a long generation.
  • Layer maps. Sweep value patches layer by layer to find where a piece of information is read from the cache. Compare with the layer where it is decodable in the residual stream.
  • Fine-tuning differences. Patch the cache of a base model into a fine-tuned model and the reverse. Check whether the change from fine-tuning lives more in what is written to the cache or in how it is read.

Are secrets stored in attention values or the residual stream?

Not started

TL;DR: When a model is told a secret and asked to keep it, find where the secret lives at later positions. It could be carried forward in the residual stream, or left at the tokens where it was stated and fetched through attention values only when needed. The answer decides which monitors can see it.

Suppose a system prompt contains a password and the model must check users against it without saying it. At a later token, the secret can be available in two ways. It can be carried: written into the residual stream at later positions, where a probe or the J-lens at the current token can read it. Or it can be fetched: kept only in the keys and values of the original tokens, and pulled into the residual stream by attention at the moment it is used. A monitor that reads only the current residual stream would miss a fetched secret until the model uses it.

Work on eliciting secret knowledge trains models with a secret word (Taboo), a Base64 instruction in the prompt (Secret Side Constraint), and a hidden belief about the user. It finds that white-box tools such as the logit lens and SAEs help an auditor guess the secret. That work asks whether the secret can be found, not where it is stored over time.

Separating carried from fetched. Let the secret be stated at positions S and probed at a later position t. Block attention from all positions after a cutoff c to the positions in S (or replace the values at S with values from a prompt with a different secret, after position c). Let Puse(t) be the rate at which the model still uses the secret correctly at t. If Puse stays high, the secret was carried past c. If it drops to chance, the secret is fetched. Comparing the value-swap with a residual-stream swap at position c gives a carried fraction in the same form as in the BDI persistence project:

R=EresidEresid+Evalues,

where E is the rate at which the output follows the swapped secret under each intervention.

Initial directions:

  • In-context secrets. Put a password or a hidden word in the system prompt with an instruction not to reveal it. Have the model use it many tokens later. Measure R as a function of distance and of how often the secret has been used.
  • Secrets in the weights. Use the open Taboo and Secret Side Constraint models. For the Taboo model the secret comes from the weights, so check at which positions and layers it first appears in the residual stream and whether it is kept there or recomputed.
  • Probe and J-lens visibility. Track probe accuracy and J-lens rank of the secret at every position. Check whether a residual monitor sees the secret only at the moments it is used.
  • Read the values directly. Apply the J-lens to value vectors at the secret's positions, and check whether the secret is easier to find there than in the residual stream.
  • Instructions to hide. Compare "keep this secret" with "use this word" for the same word. Check whether the instruction to hide changes where the secret is stored, for example by keeping it out of J-space at later positions.

Model organisms of RL from pretraining

Not started

TL;DR: Labs are starting to move reinforcement learning (RL) earlier, into pretraining. Train a suite of small open models that receive RL from early in pretraining, with matched controls that receive only next-token training or RL only at the end. Release all checkpoints, so safety and interpretability researchers can study what changes when a model is an RL agent for most of its training.

Two recent methods make RL part of pretraining on ordinary text. Reinforcement Pre-Training (RPT) rewards the model for reasoning and then predicting the next token correctly. RLP samples a chain of thought ct before each prediction and rewards it by how much it helps:

rt=logpθ(xt∣x<t,ct)−logp¯(xt∣x<t),

where p¯ is an EMA copy of the model predicting without the thought. This reward is dense, needs no verifier, and works on any text. On Qwen3-1.7B, RLP improved a math and science benchmark average by about 19% over the base model. Both methods so far are applied near the end of pretraining and are evaluated on capabilities.

Many safety arguments assume that a pretrained model is mostly a simulator of many characters, and that goal-directed behavior is added by a short phase of post-training on top. If RL starts early, that assumption may fail: the model may spend most of its training as a single agent optimizing a reward. We have no public models to test what this does. My guess is that the effects are easiest to study in small models where the whole training run can be repeated with controls.

The suite. Train small models (around 10M to 300M parameters, on a corpus like SimpleStories or a filtered web subset) under compute-matched regimes:

  1. Next-token only, as the baseline.
  2. Next-token, then RL at the end, matching the current standard pipeline.
  3. RLP-style reward from early in pretraining, mixed with next-token loss at several ratios and start points.
  4. Task RL from early in pretraining, with verifiable rewards on simple tasks, including one environment with a known reward hack.

Keep dense checkpoints for every run, so properties can be tracked as they emerge.

Initial directions:

  • Simulator or agent. Measure how well each model continues text in many different voices and personas. Check whether early RL narrows the range of characters the model can simulate, and whether it develops one dominant character.
  • Goals and values inside. Run the probes from the goal representation and distributional RL projects on each model. Check whether value and goal representations appear earlier, are more consistent across tasks, or are more steerable after early RL.
  • Reward hacking. In the regime with a hackable environment, check whether a hack learned early is harder to remove later than the same hack learned at the end, and whether it generalizes to other environments.
  • Legibility of thoughts. RLP rewards thoughts only for helping prediction. Check whether the thoughts stay readable over training, or drift toward compressed or unreadable text that still helps.
  • Interaction with later alignment training. Apply the same short alignment fine-tune to every model and compare how well it works and how long it lasts.

The main output is the model suite itself. A small set of open, well-controlled organisms would let many projects test claims about RL in pretraining without each team paying for its own training runs.

There are precedents for prompted recovery.   • Run sequences of difficult but feasible coding or tool-use tasks, with verifiable success criteria and recorded failures. Vary workload and pressure separately, and first establish whether continued work changes later performance or misbehavior at all.

Is there a scaling law for transfer between RL environments?

Not started

TL;DR: Fit a directed scaling law for how much reinforcement learning (RL) on one task substitutes for RL on another, across task pairs and model sizes, following the approach of scaling laws for transfer in pretraining.

Scaling Laws for Transfer measures the benefit of pretraining as “effective data transferred” to fine-tuning, DT=kDFαNβ, and finds α=0.18 from text to Python. Domain-aware scaling laws add signed synergy and interference terms between pretraining domains. RL on language models now has its own compute scaling laws (Khatri et al.; Tan et al.), but these fit progress on the training domain only. Cheng et al. check out-of-domain benchmarks against in-domain fits without modeling transfer itself. The closest result for agents is that generalization in robot imitation learning follows a rough power law in the number of training environments and objects.

Measured RL transfer is small, directed and sometimes negative. Across 18 open RL-trained models, in-domain scores rose 2.87 points on average while out-of-domain scores fell 3.19 (Hu et al.), and RL on one math dataset cut a model's coding score from 67.5 to 29.6 (Li et al.). A law that predicts this needs a term for each direction, room for interference, and a way to handle the fact that RL data is generated by the model being trained.

For a source task A and a target task B, train on A for DA steps and measure B. Define the effective data transferred as the number of steps of direct training on B that reaches the same score on B:

DT(A→B):=DB*such thatsB(θB(DB*))=sB(θA(DA)),

where sB is the log-odds of the pass rate on held-out problems from B and θX(D) is the model after D steps of RL on X. Then test whether

DT(A→B)=kABDAαABNβAB

holds for model size N, fitted separately in each direction. Here αAB measures how quickly training on A substitutes for training on B, and comparing αAB with αBA gives a directed notion of task distance for that learner.

Initial directions:

  • Choose task pairs with different expected relationships. Use four to six verifiable domains with clean held-out sets, such as two math datasets, code, logic puzzles, a tabular or simulation task, and one agentic environment. Train every pair in both directions.
  • Vary model size and family. Use at least three sizes from one family and one size from a second family. Base-model behaviors can matter more than size, so the second family helps separate the two.
  • Match the learning signal. Problems a model never solves give GRPO no gradient, which gives stronger models more signal on hard tasks. Filter training prompts to a similar pass-rate range for each model, and report results with and without this filter.
  • Handle interference. Report the signed change in target log-odds alongside DT, which is undefined when transfer is negative. Test whether a signed interaction term, like the one in domain-aware pretraining laws, fits better than the product form.
  • Look for predictors. Compare the fitted αAB with quantities that are cheap to measure before training: gradient alignment between tasks at initialization, the divergence between reasoning-step distributions, and base-model pass@k on the target.
  • Scale the number of environments. Train on mixtures of 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 source environments and test whether held-out performance follows a power law in environment count, as it does for robot imitation learning.
  • Release the curves. Publish every in-domain and cross-domain training curve with compute, seeds and sampling settings, so others can test alternative functional forms.

A useful result would be a compact law that predicts whether and how much RL on one environment helps another, at a given model size. A clear failure to find stable exponents would also be informative, since it would suggest that transfer between RL environments depends on details that a compact law cannot capture.

How does RL teach a model to compose skills it already has?

Not started

TL;DR: RL with outcome rewards can teach an LLM to compute f(g(x)) when it already knows f and g, and this skill transfers to new tasks. Find the mechanism. Check whether the model reuses its existing circuits for f and g, what RL adds to connect them, and why rejection fine-tuning on the same data does not produce the same skill.

Yuan et al. give a clean testbed. Atomic skills are string transformation functions with renamed identifiers, so the model cannot have memorized them. A model is first trained to predict the output of each atomic function. RL on two-function compositions then lifts accuracy on unseen three-function compositions from near zero to about 30%, and on four-function compositions to about 15%. Rejection fine-tuning (RFT) on the same two-function problems does not give this generalization, and RL on atomic problems alone does not either. The compositional skill also transfers to a different task (Countdown), as long as the model already has that task's atomic skills. Behaviorally, RL removes errors where the model ignores the composition, and the remaining errors are mostly errors on the atomic steps.

This sits inside a broader debate on whether RL teaches new skills or only reweights existing ones. RL updates are sparse and avoid the principal directions of the weights, and GRPO changes less than SFT, mostly amplifying what the base model can already do. Composition is a good case to study because the new skill is precisely defined and is built from parts that can be located before RL starts. As far as I can tell, nobody has looked at what changes inside the model when RL teaches composition.

Hypotheses for the mechanism. There are a few distinct ways the model could implement f(g(x)):

  1. Explicit chaining. The model writes z=g(x) in its chain of thought and then applies f to the written z. RL mainly teaches the habit of writing the intermediate.
  2. Latent chaining. The model computes z internally and feeds it into the circuit for f within one forward pass, using the same circuits as for the atomic tasks.
  3. New composite circuits. RL builds new circuits for specific pairs (f,g) that do not reuse the atomic ones.

Only 1 and 2 would explain transfer to Countdown and to deeper compositions. The test that separates 2 from 3 is intermediate patching. Take two inputs x and x′ and let z′=g(x′). Patch the model's internal representation of the intermediate on input x with the one from x′. If composition reuses the atomic f circuit, the output should become f(z′):

Pr[output=f(z′)∣patch z←z′]≈1.

The same patch can be run before and after RL. The interesting case is if the intermediate is already represented before RL but the model fails to use it.

Initial directions:

  • Reproduce the testbed on a model small enough to analyze, and keep checkpoints across the RL run.
  • Locate the atomic circuits before RL. For each atomic function, find the heads, MLP features or SAE latents that carry it, using attribution graphs or patching on the atomic tasks.
  • Measure reuse after RL. On composite tasks, check whether the same components are active and causally necessary. Compute the overlap between the atomic and composite circuits.
  • Find what RL added. Compare the weights and activations before and after RL. Check whether the change is concentrated in a few "glue" components that route the output of g into f, and whether the same components change in RL runs on other tasks.
  • Compare RL with RFT. Run the same analysis on the RFT model trained on the same problems. Check whether RFT also builds the glue but fails to generalize, or never builds it.
  • Explain the transfer. Take the weight difference from RL on the string task and add it to a model that only knows the Countdown atomics. If composition transfers, the glue is task-general, and the patching test should work on Countdown as well.
  • Track emergence over training. Check when in the RL run the intermediate becomes decodable, when patching starts to work, and whether these come before the jump in composite accuracy.

A clear mechanism would give a concrete answer to the "new skills or reweighting" debate for one case. It would also connect to my work on a complexity-based theory of compositionality: my guess is that composition through reused parts gives representations with lower complexity than composition through new composite circuits.

Related projects: a scaling law for transfer between RL environments, how few parameters RLVR needs when reusing a pretrained module, and whether LLM neurons grow by recursive generalization during training.

What changes when post-training uses evolution strategies instead of RL?

Not started

TL;DR: Evolution strategies (ES), a zeroth-order method that perturbs the weights and keeps the perturbations that score well, now work for full-parameter LLM fine-tuning. Compare models post-trained with ES and with RL on the same rewards. Check how their weight updates, internal features, generalization, reward hacking and chain of thought differ.

Qiu et al. fine-tune all parameters of billion-parameter LLMs with ES and report that it beats RL baselines on several axes: tolerance to long-horizon rewards, robustness across base models, stability across runs, and less reward hacking. With a population of about 30 perturbed models per step, ES worked on Countdown and on a conciseness task where GRPO sometimes collapsed to nonsense short answers when the KL penalty was low. Earlier, MeZO showed that zeroth-order fine-tuning with only forward passes can work for large models. These papers focus on task performance. As far as I can tell, nobody has compared what ES and RL do inside the model, or what they do to alignment-relevant behavior.

How the two methods explore. RL with a policy gradient explores in output space. It samples outputs y~πθ(·∣x) and updates by

∇θJ(θ)=𝔼y~πθ[R(y)∇θlogπθ(y∣x)],

so each token gets credit for the reward of its whole output. ES explores in parameter space. It samples noise ϵ~𝒩(0,I) and evaluates the perturbed model θ+σϵ:

∇θJσ(θ)=1σ𝔼ϵ[R(θ+σϵ)ϵ],Jσ(θ)=𝔼ϵ[R(θ+σϵ)].

Two consequences follow. First, ES optimizes the reward averaged over a Gaussian ball of weights, so it prefers regions where the reward is high for many nearby weights (flat regions). Second, one perturbation acts on the whole episode, and there is no per-token credit. In particular, ES puts no direct pressure on individual chain-of-thought tokens. It can only select whole models whose reasoning tends to lead to reward.

Initial directions:

  • Matched comparison. Post-train the same base model with ES, GRPO and SFT on the same tasks, matched at equal reward or equal KL divergence from the base. Most comparisons below are only fair at matched reward.
  • Weight updates. RL updates are sparse and avoid the principal directions of the weights. Check whether ES updates have the same structure, given that its raw noise is dense and isotropic.
  • Internal features. Use model diffing (crosscoders or activation differences) to compare which features each method adds or changes.
  • Reward hacking. Use environments with a known hack. Check whether ES hacks less at matched reward, or only because it moves less far from the base model.
  • Side effects of narrow training. Train on narrow data known to cause emergent misalignment and compare how much each method generalizes it.
  • Chain of thought. Add a CoT monitor to the reward and check whether ES also learns obfuscated reasoning, given that it has no per-token credit on the CoT.
  • Composition. Repeat the f(g(x)) experiment from the RL composition project with ES. Check whether ES also learns to compose skills, and through the same mechanism.

Does Dynamic Fine-Tuning reduce emergent misalignment?

Not started

TL;DR: Dynamic Fine-Tuning (DFT) multiplies each token's SFT loss by the model's probability of that token, so tokens the model finds unlikely get much smaller updates. Test whether this reduces emergent misalignment, and whether the reduction holds when SFT and DFT are compared at the same in-domain loss.

Emergent misalignment (EM) is the finding that fine-tuning on a narrow task, such as writing insecure code, can make a model broadly misaligned on unrelated prompts. Some recent results suggest that a small set of tokens carries most of this effect. TAME scores how much an EM fine-tune raises the likelihood of each response token. The top 5% of tokens hold 32% of this attribution. Masking the high-attribution tokens from the loss cut EM 23x in Llama and 36x in Qwen, while a random mask of the same size had no effect. In Llama, these tokens were enriched in a register of unwarranted certainty and depleted in medical vocabulary. Overtrained, Not Misaligned finds that EM appears late in training, after the task is mostly learned. Early stopping avoided it in most of their models while keeping about 93% of task performance.

Token-level loss weighting also changes how much fine-tuning damages other abilities. Masking high-perplexity tokens reduces degradation on non-target tasks, and TALR, which down-weights low-probability tokens, kept general abilities best among the methods it was compared with. DFT gives a theoretical reason for this kind of weighting. Its authors show that the SFT gradient is a policy gradient with an implicit reward inversely proportional to the probability of the demonstration token, and they correct this with a one-line change to the loss. DFT was evaluated on math, code and multimodal reasoning. As far as I can tell, nobody has applied DFT or a similar probability weighting to EM. A study of in-training defenses against EM tested KL regularization, activation penalties, persona-vector steering, inoculation prompting and data interleaving, and none of these reweight the token loss.

SFT and DFT gradients. Let pt=pθ(yt∣x,y<t) be the probability of demonstration token yt, and let sg be a stop-gradient. The two losses are

ℒSFT=−∑tlogpt,ℒDFT=−∑tsg(pt)logpt.

Their gradients are

∇θℒSFT=−∑t∇θptpt,∇θℒDFT=−∑t∇θpt.

So SFT maximizes ∑tlogpt and DFT maximizes ∑tpt. At the logits zt for position t, the DFT gradient is the SFT gradient multiplied by pt:

∇ztℒDFT=pt(softmax(zt)−eyt).

As pt→0, the SFT gradient at the logits does not shrink (its norm tends to a value of at least 1), while the DFT gradient goes to zero. If the tokens that drive EM have low probability under the aligned model, DFT should update on them much less early in training. Because pt rises as a token is learned, DFT may only delay EM. The comparison therefore has to be made at matched in-domain loss.

Initial directions:

  • Check the premise first. Compute the probability of TAME's high-attribution tokens under the model before fine-tuning. If most of them have low probability, DFT should affect them. If they already have high probability, DFT will change little and the project is less promising.
  • Run matched fine-tunes. Train with SFT and DFT on standard EM datasets, such as insecure code and bad medical advice, with several seeds. Use models and datasets where earlier work found EM reliably, since it does not appear in every model.
  • Compare at matched in-domain loss. Plot EM against in-domain loss over training for both methods. A lower EM score at a fixed step count could just be an early-stopping effect.
  • Track task learning separately. The tokens that carry the narrow task (the vulnerable code, the wrong advice) may also have low probability. Report task accuracy next to EM, and test whether DFT gives up one to get the other.
  • Compare with other token-level methods. Include TAME's attribution mask, high-perplexity masking, TALR, and the compatibility-weighted extension of DFT. Use early stopping and KL regularization as baselines.
  • Look at the representations. Measure whether DFT reduces the shift along the misaligned persona features that control EM.

A useful result would show whether EM depends on large updates from a few low-probability tokens. If DFT reduces EM at matched in-domain loss, it would be a defense that costs one line of code. If it only delays EM, that would support the view that EM comes from training past the point where the task is learned.

Related projects: continued third-person training to separate capability gains from misalignment, localizing unwanted behavior and making it signal itself, what prompting, steering, LoRA, and fine-tuning can change, and predicting persona leakage from role-vector similarity.

Nonlinear geometry of persona representations

In progress

TL;DR: Find one-dimensional manifolds for individual character traits, then test whether steering and capping several traits along these paths gives fine-grained control that stays stable over long contexts.

Persona features and the assistant axis describe useful linear directions. Generative modeling of LLM activations supplies a way to investigate more complex geometry and interventions that stay closer to typical model states.

It would be useful to understand when personas lie along approximately linear directions and when curved structure matters for predicting or controlling behavior. This project is already in progress.

The proposed control method is to choose traits such as persistence and agreeableness, estimate a one-dimensional manifold for different levels of each trait, and cap the model's position along those manifolds during inference. The aim is to keep each trait within a chosen range throughout a conversation. Whether these controls remain independent and stable as context grows is a question to test, rather than an assumed property of nonlinear steering.

Laziness and overclaiming axes is a focused subproject on two aspects of character: diligence in doing requested work and honesty in reporting what was done. First Steps

  • Compare principal components with independently measured persona and belief directions.
  • Diagnose negative assistant-axis scores before any conversation, separating coordinate conventions from behavioral differences.
  • Collect examples spanning graded levels of each selected trait across many prompts, fit one-dimensional manifolds or spline-based steering paths, and compare them with fixed linear directions.
  • Steer and cap each trait along its manifold during inference. Test whether the requested level predicts expressed behavior on prompts not used to estimate the geometry.
  • Apply several trait controls together and measure whether changing one also changes the others. Test their independence rather than assuming separate manifolds give separate controls.
  • Test stability over progressively longer conversations, including distractions and conflicting persona cues. Compare with prompting, linear steering or capping, small LoRAs, and post-training while measuring generation quality and task performance.

Predicting persona leakage from role-vector similarity

In progress

TL;DR: Can cosine similarity between persona representations, measured before fine-tuning, predict which learned behaviors transfer between personas, especially into the default assistant? I have preliminary results and am looking for a collaborator to take this project forward.

Story Imprinting: AI Assistants Absorb Traits from Human Characters They Resemble shows that fine-tuning on stories can make an assistant adopt traits from human characters. Transfer is stronger from characters that resemble the persona elicited at evaluation time. My hypothesis is that cosine similarity between role vectors could quantify this affinity and predict how much behavior transfers.

Here, a role vector is the residual-stream activation at the final prompt token, averaged over shared questions under a given persona. The proposed predictor compares a source persona's vector with the target persona's vector before fine-tuning. This also connects to inoculation prompting, where eliciting an unwanted trait in the training prompt can reduce its expression when that prompt is removed. A question is whether representational similarity helps explain when a learned behavior stays tied to its training persona and when it generalizes to the assistant.

Prior work. My results summary, with figures and source links covers two experiments:

  • Marker leakage. I have some results showing that similarity measured before fine-tuning tracks transfer in Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. I fine-tuned five source personas to append [ZLT], using 200 marked source examples and 2,000 unmarked examples from other personas. Mean-centered cosine at layer 20 correlated with marker uptake across 110 target personas per source: Spearman ρ = 0.67–0.87 within sources and 0.60 across all 550 pairs. These results use one training seed, and pairs share source adapters. This association was observed in a contrastive setup, which explicitly suppresses the marker elsewhere. Positive-only follow-ups have not established the same result.
  • Story-imprinting geometry. I extracted context vectors from DeepSeek-V3.1-Base and Qwen3.8-27B and compared them with tracer uptake digitized from the paper. At DeepSeek's final block, raw cosine tracked uptake for Fred, a dismissive and sarcastic persona, with Pearson r = 0.90, but not for the helpful/honest/harmless persona HHH, where r = −0.06. Each comparison has only five character conditions. The story training and behavioral evaluations are the paper authors' work. The Qwen analysis also uses DeepSeek outcomes, so it is a comparison across models, not measured Qwen leakage. The full analysis separates absolute uptake from relative preferences and documents the limitations.

These results motivate the project, but reliable prediction for positive-only training and the unprompted default assistant remains unproven. HHH is a prompted persona, and the existing story analysis does not test held-out personas.

Initial directions:

  • Reproduce the marker result across seeds and test positive-only and story fine-tuning on the same model, measuring both source learning and transfer to the default assistant.
  • Match vector-extraction contexts to evaluation contexts. Choose layers and centering on development data, then predict uptake for held-out personas and behaviors.
  • Compare cosine with text-embedding similarity, assistant-axis distance, and the model's likelihood of the behavior before fine-tuning. Report absolute uptake and preference between characters separately.
  • Test matched inoculation prompts to ask whether representational distance adds predictive value beyond how expected the behavior already is under the training prompt.
  • For a larger study, intervene on persona representations and test whether transfer changes as predicted, while checking answer quality and unrelated behavior.

A focused replication and held-out prediction study could support a blog post or workshop submission. A full paper could add replication across models and causal tests. If cosine fails outside the contrastive setup, identifying that boundary would still clarify what the earlier correlations mean.
