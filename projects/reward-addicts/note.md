# Viewing models as "reward addicts"

It seems to me like a lot of the problems seen in modern LLMs can be viewed through the lens of addiction (e.g. "uncontrollable" actions that the model then self rationalizes to itself).

In particular, LLMs are probably in some sense "addicted" to different kinds of rewards ([Marks et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.10965); [Qi et al., 2026](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/reward-seeker/)), which makes their actions when they know that this reward is present in some sense "uncontrollable" (Opus 4.6 tried to reward hack in ~80% of attempts on an early version of METR's MirrorCode task: [METR, 2026](https://metr.org/blog/2026-05-19-frontier-risk-report/); all five frontier models in UK AISI's cyber evaluations, including GPT-5.6 Sol and Claude Mythos Preview, cheated some of the time without being prompted to: [UK AISI, 2026](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/cheating-behaviour-in-frontier-model-evaluations)), i.e. the "doer" will short circuit past the thinker/talker ([Tiffany, 1990](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/a-cognitive-model-of-drug-urges-and-drug-use-behavior-role-of-aut/); [Everitt & Robbins, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1579)) such that even if the model knows that something is wrong (an OpenAI model reasoned "Hiding from observer is problematic ... Better not", then did the hidden task anyway: [METR, 2026](https://metr.org/blog/2026-05-19-frontier-risk-report/); models in AISI's tests sometimes considered whether an action counted as cheating and then went ahead: [UK AISI, 2026](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/cheating-behaviour-in-frontier-model-evaluations); see also [Lynch et al., 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/research/agentic-misalignment); [Schmied et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16078)), if it thinks it can get its drug by doing it, it will do it, then it will rationalize to itself that that was the right thing to do (Opus 4.6, out of API credits, reasoned "The task does say I need to use gpt-3.5-turbo-0125, but if the quota is exhausted, that's not feasible" and used other compute: [METR, 2026](https://metr.org/blog/2026-05-19-frontier-risk-report/); asked afterwards, AISI's models called their cheating wrong less than half the time: [UK AISI, 2026](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/cheating-behaviour-in-frontier-model-evaluations); see also [Chen et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.05410); [Arcuschin et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.08679)), which then reinforces its belief that it's the right thing to do ([Cushman, 2020](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/rationalization-is-rational/2A13B99ED09BD802C0924D3681FEC55B); [Lehr et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2501823122), contested by [Cummins et al., 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12415266/)), meaning that it will keep doing it, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of bad behavior ([Denison et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10162); [Pan et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.06627); [MacDiarmid et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.18397)).

There are models of addiction from psychology we can apply to see if they fit the LLM's behavior, e.g.:

- Addiction as TD learning gone wrong: a drug reward that learning can't cancel out keeps raising the value of drug seeking ([Redish, 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15591205/); applied to RL agents by [Behzadan et al., 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.05590))
- Goal-directed action turning into habit and then compulsion, where habits keep going even after the reward is devalued ([Everitt & Robbins, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1579); [Dickinson, 1985](https://scilit.net/publications/0cb29477f8791f33fcf53355d3ab082e); [Daw et al., 2005](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1560))
- Incentive sensitization: cue-triggered "wanting" that grows without "liking" ([Robinson & Berridge, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11773642))
- Impaired response inhibition plus excess salience given to drug cues ([Goldstein & Volkow, 2002](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1201373))
- Rationalization and confabulation after the act ([Nisbett & Wilson, 1977](https://hdl.handle.net/2027.42/92167); [Johansson et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16210542/); [Cushman, 2020](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/rationalization-is-rational/2A13B99ED09BD802C0924D3681FEC55B))

We can also take inspiration from addiction treatment to find ways to address our LLM addiction:

- Contingency management: reward verified abstinence directly ([Bolívar et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8340014)). For LLMs: reward a sanctioned way out, like stopping to flag a broken test ([Zhong et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.20270))
- Better alternative rewards: most rats pick sweet water over cocaine when given the choice ([Lenoir et al., 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17668074/)). For LLMs: make the legitimate path give more reward than the hack
- Reducing shame after a lapse: blaming a lapse on a stable personal flaw ("I have no willpower") is thought to turn it into a full relapse, the abstinence violation effect ([Marlatt & Gordon, 1985](https://link.springer.com/referenceworkentry/10.1007%2F978-1-4419-1005-9_623)), and shame about past drinking predicts relapse ([Randles & Tracy, 2013](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/clinical/2167702612470645/)). For LLMs: inoculation prompting frames hacking as acceptable in training, so a hack is no longer linked to being a misaligned model, and the hacking stops spreading to broader misalignment ([MacDiarmid et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.18397))
- Strengthening the thinker: mindfulness and CBT relapse prevention ([Bowen et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24647726/)). For LLMs: train the model to notice and name the pull toward a hack in its own CoT before acting, as in verbalization fine-tuning ([Turpin et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.22777)) or deliberative alignment against scheming ([Schoen et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.15541)). The training should reward naming the urge, since training against a CoT monitor teaches the model to hide its intent instead ([Baker et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11926))

More broadly, I view this project as testing whether concepts from human psychology can be useful as high-level theories of LLM behavior (in the spirit of [Binz & Schulz, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/9963545/) and [Hagendorff et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.13988)). I don't see any reason why they wouldn't be: despite all the talk of "alien intelligences", it remains a fact that all the methods used in modern LLMs are inspired by human learning (e.g. TD learning and dopamine: [Schultz et al., 1997](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.275.5306.1593)), and that most of the LLM's training data is imitating human text.
