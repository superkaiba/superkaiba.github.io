# Is there a scaling law for transfer between RL environments?

**TL;DR:** Fit a directed scaling law for how much reinforcement learning (RL) on one task substitutes for RL on another, across task pairs and model sizes, following the approach of scaling laws for transfer in pretraining.

[Scaling Laws for Transfer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.01293) measures the benefit of pretraining as “effective data transferred” to fine-tuning, $D_T = k\,D_F^{\alpha}N^{\beta}$, and finds $\alpha = 0.18$ from text to Python. [Domain-aware scaling laws](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.11052) add signed synergy and interference terms between pretraining domains. RL on language models now has its own compute scaling laws ([Khatri et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13786); [Tan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25300)), but these fit progress on the training domain only. [Cheng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04158) check out-of-domain benchmarks against in-domain fits without modeling transfer itself. The closest result for agents is that generalization in robot imitation learning follows a [rough power law in the number of training environments and objects](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18647).

Measured RL transfer is small, directed and sometimes negative. Across 18 open RL-trained models, in-domain scores rose 2.87 points on average while out-of-domain scores fell 3.19 ([Hu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.19733)), and RL on one math dataset cut a model's coding score from 67.5 to 29.6 ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.17512)). A law that predicts this needs a term for each direction, room for interference, and a way to handle the fact that RL data is generated by the model being trained.

For a source task $A$ and a target task $B$, train on $A$ for $D_A$ steps and measure $B$. Define the effective data transferred as the number of steps of direct training on $B$ that reaches the same score on $B$:

$$
D_T(A \to B) := D_B^{*} \quad \text{such that} \quad s_B\big(\theta_B(D_B^{*})\big) = s_B\big(\theta_A(D_A)\big),
$$

where $s_B$ is the log-odds of the pass rate on held-out problems from $B$ and $\theta_X(D)$ is the model after $D$ steps of RL on $X$. Then test whether

$$
D_T(A \to B) = k_{AB}\, D_A^{\alpha_{AB}}\, N^{\beta_{AB}}
$$

holds for model size $N$, fitted separately in each direction. Here $\alpha_{AB}$ measures how quickly training on $A$ substitutes for training on $B$, and comparing $\alpha_{AB}$ with $\alpha_{BA}$ gives a directed notion of task distance for that learner.

**Initial directions:**

- **Choose task pairs with different expected relationships.** Use four to six verifiable domains with clean held-out sets, such as two math datasets, code, logic puzzles, a tabular or simulation task, and one agentic environment. Train every pair in both directions.
- **Vary model size and family.** Use at least three sizes from one family and one size from a second family. [Base-model behaviors can matter more than size](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01307), so the second family helps separate the two.
- **Match the learning signal.** Problems a model never solves give GRPO no gradient, which gives stronger models more signal on hard tasks. Filter training prompts to a similar pass-rate range for each model, and report results with and without this filter.
- **Handle interference.** Report the signed change in target log-odds alongside $D_T$, which is undefined when transfer is negative. Test whether a signed interaction term, like the one in domain-aware pretraining laws, fits better than the product form.
- **Look for predictors.** Compare the fitted $\alpha_{AB}$ with quantities that are cheap to measure before training: gradient alignment between tasks at initialization, the [divergence between reasoning-step distributions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.19733), and base-model pass@k on the target.
- **Scale the number of environments.** Train on mixtures of 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 source environments and test whether held-out performance follows a power law in environment count, as it does for robot imitation learning.
- **Release the curves.** Publish every in-domain and cross-domain training curve with compute, seeds and sampling settings, so others can test alternative functional forms.

A useful result would be a compact law that predicts whether and how much RL on one environment helps another, at a given model size. A clear failure to find stable exponents would also be informative, since it would suggest that transfer between RL environments depends on details that a compact law cannot capture.
