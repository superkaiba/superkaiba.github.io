Chasing the Ballmer Peak

On feeling brilliant, losing your bearings, and knowing when to stop.

Thomas JiralerspongSeptember 20, 20268 min read

If you're reading this, you've probably heard of the Ballmer peak. Randall Munroe's xkcd comic proposes a very specific level of drunkenness that supposedly gives you superhuman programming ability. Too little alcohol and you're leaving gains on the table. Too much and you become useless.

Someone actually tried to find it by drinking and doing LeetCode for SIGBOVIK, a comedy research conference. No superhuman peak emerged. I met the person who did it. That was a fun conversation.

So no, I'm not going to claim that the Ballmer Peak is real. Also, the original graph gets worse before its ridiculous spike. For this post, I'm going to use the simpler version I had in my head: you get better up to a point, and then you fall off a cliff.

But there's a curve missing from the comic that I think is more interesting.

How good you think you are

Let's generalize this to drugs more broadly. There's how well you're actually doing, and how well you think you're doing.

Anecdotally, alcohol, weed and shrooms can make you feel super smart, or like you can do anything. There's the “OMG I'M A GENIUS” insight, and there's the drunk person who suddenly thinks they can do a backflip. Neither feeling is a particularly good evaluation of your abilities.

Then you write down your brilliant insight and reread it the next day, only to realize it's gibberish. Or you try the backflip and end up in the emergency room (definitely not speaking from personal experience...).

Studies find versions of this gap too. In a bar-based alcohol study, participants overestimated their scores on reasoning problems. In a sober follow-up, they performed better but underestimated their scores. The study didn't cleanly separate alcohol from the bar setting. In a cannabis experiment, people rated their ideas as more creative without independent judges finding an improvement. And in a psilocybin study, people felt more spontaneous insight during the acute effects while doing worse on deliberate creativity tasks.

With alcohol and other drugs, there's usually an opportunity to look again once the acute effects have worn off. You reread the note you were so excited about and discover that “everything is connected because everything is everything” might need a little more work before submission to Nature.

Now imagine a drug that never wore off.

It doesn't otherwise stop you from functioning in the world. At first, it really does make you more capable. But past a certain point, your actual capability starts to decline while your confidence keeps going up. And since it never wears off, you don't get that automatic opportunity to come back and evaluate your work from outside the experience.

Why would you stop taking it? As far as you can tell, you're doing better than ever. Unless something outside that loop gives you a reliable reality check, you could keep going until you were producing complete garbage and feeling great about it.

The AI Ballmer Peak

I think AI can create a version of this loop.

At first, the productivity gains are real. You spend less time fighting with an API, writing boilerplate, or setting up a plot. You can get to the interesting part of the problem much faster. Using too little AI really can mean leaving a lot of useful work undone.

But as you delegate more, you can also lose the contact with the work that lets you judge whether it's any good.

With alcohol, the substance acts on your brain. With AI, the mechanism I'm worried about is that you become less able to evaluate the work because you understand less of what actually happened. You can still think clearly about the summary in front of you. The problem is that the summary may have become almost your entire relationship with the project.

I'm going to focus on AI research because that's what I have experience with, but I suspect this extends to a lot of other things.

Imagine the following progression.

At first, you tell your coding agent: “Implement this part of the code.” You don't check every line. Later, some results don't make sense, so you go back and investigate, or ask the agent to investigate. It comes back with “Oh, there's a really dumb bug.” You fix it and rerun.

This isn't an ideal process, but you're still close enough to the work to notice that something is wrong. You know what the experiment is supposed to do. You have expectations about its behavior. The weird result means something to you.

As the models get better, the obvious bugs become less frequent. You start saying: “Run this experiment.” Now you're delegating more of the setup and making fewer of the decisions yourself. You still look at the results, and sometimes something seems off. You dig into the experiment and find that the agent made a bunch of choices that don't make sense.

Eventually, you're saying: “Test this hypothesis.”

The agent decides how to operationalize it, chooses the data and baselines, writes the code, runs the experiments, and interprets the results. It comes back with “The hypothesis is supported!” or “The hypothesis is false!”

And you're like, oh okay.

The problem is that, somewhere along this progression, you may have lost the understanding that would let you judge whether this was a good test of the hypothesis in the first place. You didn't see the examples. You didn't think through the baseline. You didn't notice the strange behavior in the first few runs. All of those encounters with the work used to help you build a picture of what was going on.

Now you have a report.

This is why “I'll just review the output” can be less reassuring than it sounds. Reviewing a conclusion requires some understanding of what would make it true or false. If you've delegated the process that would have given you that understanding, the review can turn into checking whether the explanation sounds reasonable.

And LLMs are very good at sounding reasonable.

There is a related argument in Messeri and Crockett's paper on AI and illusions of understanding in scientific research: tools that let us produce more can also leave us thinking we understand more than we do. What worries me is how naturally you can get there, one useful delegation at a time.

You don't have to write every line of code yourself to understand your research. Higher-level delegation can work very well. But you still need some way of maintaining contact with the evidence. Otherwise, “I can't see anything wrong with this” starts to mean “I no longer know enough about this to see what's wrong.”

In research, falling off the cliff can still look productive: lots of experiments, clean figures, even an entire paper. The code runs and the explanation sounds reasonable, but the experiment doesn't actually test the claim. That's what I mean by slop: you keep generating the artifacts of research after you've stopped doing the thinking that makes them useful.

The peak keeps moving

This is the annoying part.

Even if you figure out a good amount of AI use for yourself, a new model comes out. Or you switch projects. Or the task changes from implementing a well-understood method to deciding whether a result means anything.

The right amount of delegation depends on what the model can do, what you understand, and how good your checks are. There isn't a permanent rule like “delegate 60% of your research.”

Better models can make it reasonable to delegate more. They can also make it easier to stop paying attention, because the outputs keep looking good. You have to keep figuring out whether your understanding and your checks are keeping up.

And unlike alcohol, closing the chat doesn't automatically restore what you missed. If you never understood the experiment, you still don't understand it the next morning. You have to go back and do some of that work.

AI Users Anonymous

I think part of what makes this so hard is that the feeling itself is addictive. You ask a question and get an insight. You suggest an experiment and get a result. You wonder if something is possible and get a plan for doing it. There is always another prompt that might make you feel like you've moved the project forward.

You can end up chasing that feeling even when you're no longer making much progress. That self-reinforcing confidence is part of what I mean here by “LLM psychosis”.

I've dealt with this too, and I'm sorry to the collaborators it affected.

There is something funny about this in San Francisco. People here put a lot of effort into avoiding alcohol. I once went to a mandatory drinking party: if you attended, you had to drink. Apparently, even drinking needs an explicit coordination mechanism here.

Meanwhile, we have another substance that lets us feel insightful and productive for hours, and using more of it can look like being more serious about our work. I can see the appeal. It's a much easier habit to justify.

The funny thing is that Randall Munroe's original comic already contains the warning:

The important part is careful calibration. Unlimited tokens can remove the pause to ask: “Is this a good use of these tokens?” You can run every experiment and delegate judging the results. You can think even less.

But unlimited tokens don't mean unlimited judgment. It's better to leave a little productivity on the table than fall off the cliff without noticing.
